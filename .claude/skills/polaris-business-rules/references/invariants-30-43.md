# Business rules 30–59 — the invariants

Each rule as CLAUDE.md stated it: the invariant plus a pointer into the narrative file (`narrative-25-35.md` / `narrative-36-43.md`), which records the incident or constraint that forced it. Rule numbers are a stable citation key — never renumber; retire a rule in place and give a new one the next free number.

30. **Confirmation is the configured cadence's job; ICMP only fills in the loss ratio** — `monitorStatus` reaches `down` when N missed polls are OUTSTANDING against the operator's configured transport at their configured `intervalSeconds`. **The counter is a LEAKY BUCKET WITH A CEILING, not a run length and not an unbounded debt**: a miss adds one to `consecutiveFailures`, an answered poll takes one back (floored at 0), and the level **LOCKS at `bucketCapFor(N, recoveryPolls)`** — the larger of the missed-poll count and the covering automation's reset count — the moment a miss carries it to N. The whole machine is two pure functions in `utils/monitorStatus.ts`, `nextFailureBucket` + `monitorStatusFor`, which the probe path CALLS rather than reimplements. The verdict: `cf == 0` is `up`, an **ANSWERED probe is `recovering` whatever the level**, and a MISS is `down` at or above N and `warning` below it — the level decides what a MISS means, the outcome decides everything else. So time-to-down is `N × intervalSeconds` for a device that stops answering and longer for one that flaps; **recovery costs exactly the cap, however long the outage ran**; and a miss anywhere in the climb re-locks the bucket, so a flapping device cannot walk itself out of an outage. **The cap and the always-recovering answered branch both landed 2026-09-01, and both were bugs.** Unbounded, a device dark overnight at a 60 s cadence reached `cf ≈ 480` and then owed 480 answered polls — eight hours — before it could read `up`, with its down alert repeating and escalating throughout, because `DOWN_ALERT_HOLDING_STATES` holds that alert until `up`. And with the answered branch BELOW the threshold test, a probe that answered while the bucket was still at or above N read `down` — which the response-time chart has no way to paint on an `ok` point, so it fell through to the plain series green and an outage drew red → GREEN → blue → green, reading as two recoveries. One lucky packet still cannot clear the alert: `recovering` is a holding state. `consecutiveSuccesses` is still maintained and still charted but **decides nothing** — the bucket's own level is the whole of the verdict AND of the recovery arithmetic, which is why `owesRecoveryConfirmation` is gone and `Asset.awaitingRecoveryConfirm` is dormant (business rule 36). Pre-ceiling rows self-heal on their next probe of either outcome (`nextFailureBucket` clamps before it moves the level) and are swept once at boot by `jobs/clampFailureBucket.ts`. **N is the covering automation's `missedPolls` (default 3), not a Monitor Settings value** — see business rule 36; `failureThreshold` on the settings tiers is dormant. **Nor is the COLOUR fixed**: `down` is drawn in that automation's own severity (`downSeverityHex` / `PolarisChartSeverity.downColorOf`), so red is what `critical` looks like rather than what Down means. Down detection was the only poll-counted decision until 2026-08-29, when every other trigger's hold and reset became counts too (rule 19) — this one still stands apart in WHERE it is counted: at the probe, which is the poll, rather than in the rules engine. The CADENCE half is unchanged: it still comes from the monitor-settings hierarchy, and the figure the monitor-settings card reports is the figure you get. `resolveProbeIntervalSec` applies **exactly one clamp**: dependency suppression (parent dark ⇒ 2× interval). **A probe can also decline to be a reading**: `ProbeResult.skipped` writes no sample and moves neither counter — it bumps `lastMonitorAt` alone, so the cadence keeps its spacing. Two sources: the vCenter probe when vCenter itself is unreachable, and **`responseTimePolling === "disabled"`**, which is the operator saying don't poll. The second was a *failure* until 2026-08-28: every other stream's publisher checked its resolved method before queueing and the probe's did not, so `disabled` reached `probeAsset`'s dispatch, fell past every branch to the unknown-method error, and was recorded as a missed poll — switching Response Time off drove the asset to `down`. Both publishers (`computeDueWork` + `jobs/monitorAssets`, the lockstep pair) now gate on the shared `responseTimeProbeShouldQueue`, and `probeAsset` returns `skipped` as the backstop for `/probe-now` and for a `disabled` sitting in a JSON tier no publisher gate can see. A skip is not a miss, so N consecutive misses still means N times the transport actually answered nothing about a device it could reach. **Nor is a re-queued job a second reading** (2026-09-10). The stately probe queue lets one job queue behind an ACTIVE job with the same key, and a batched ICMP chunk (or a probe to a dark host) outlives the 5 s publisher tick. The same poll therefore ran twice, seconds apart: two samples per cycle, and two misses toward N. Jobs now carry the resolved interval and the worker re-runs the due check at pickup (`probeStillDue`); a duplicate records nothing, not even `lastMonitorAt`. The ICMP loss sweep **never calls `recordProbeResult`** (ICMP does not authenticate the device it reaches), so it informs the loss ratio and can never move `monitorStatus`. It replaced the per-asset sampler on 2026-09-01: that one ran only while an asset looked unhealthy, which was itself a sampling bias (rule 29d) and is why it shipped disabled; the sweep is uniform — every eligible asset, every cycle, `down` and dependency-suppressed included — and ships enabled, batched through `utils/burstPing.ts` so a 2000-asset fleet costs four process spawns rather than two thousand. `probeKind` splits the readers: only `probeLossQuery` and the alert-email loss chart read all kinds — every response-time reader, and every rollup, filters to primary. → [narrative-25-35.md#rule-30](narrative-25-35.md#rule-30)

31. **The login page is the way back in, so restricting it is opt-in and must refuse to lock you out** — `loginAccessConfig` (default **off**) restricts local login by source IP via `utils/ipScope.ts`. It gates **both halves or neither** — `/login.html` AND `POST /auth/login` + `/auth/login/totp` — because the form is a plain POST to a JSON API. The page is **dropped** (socket destroyed, stealth posture); the API answers the **same generic 401** a wrong password gets, and writes `auth.login.blocked_source`. **SSO is never gated** (that's what makes this survivable) — and a RATE LIMITER counts as a gate twice over: `/auth/azure/login` shared the password form's 10-per-15-min budget until 2026-09-16 and now carries `ssoEntryLimiter` at the route, and **an SSO entry ceiling must never sit below the callback ceiling behind it**, because one sign-in is exactly one entry then one callback from the same IP — a tighter entry makes the callback's deliberately NAT-sized allowance unreachable. **LDAP is** gated, sharing the login route. It **fails open** on a settings read error, and the PUT refuses a scope excluding the caller's own `req.ip`. Only as true as `req.ip` — the GET returns `callerIp` for exactly that reason. → [narrative-25-35.md#rule-31](narrative-25-35.md#rule-31)

32. **A reset condition answers "what has to become true again", so it starts as the trigger inverted — and it resolves where the alert lives** — the custom-condition mode is offered on every trigger with a continuous condition (`TRIGGER_TYPES_WITH_RESET_CONDITIONS`; `event`/`change` are refused), pre-seeded with the trigger inverted (`invertedLeaf`, and `invertedTree` applying De Morgan so the result stays and/or-only). Four decisions carry it: (a) while firing, **the tree is the sole recovery authority** — evaluated in its own post-loop pass that re-reads firing rows from the DB; (b) resolution is **dimension-first, with the per-asset fallback reserved for a leaf in ANOTHER dimension space** (`dimensionSpaceOf`) — so a per-port alert clears independently while a device-wide leaf (CPU, at dimKey "") clears them together, and a leaf that speaks the firing row's OWN vocabulary and has no reading for that component says nothing rather than letting a sibling component answer for it (a reset tree never fires on absent evidence, so the alert stands). A reset leaf also **inherits the trigger's port coverage**: an automation whose trigger reads unpinned PoE ports under rule 57's carve-out resolves its reset leaves over the same ports, because the seeded leaf (`poeStatus != fault`) can never qualify for that carve-out on its own terms. Before both halves landed, a PoE-fault alert on an unpinned port was cleared by the first healthy port on the switch — or, on a switch with nothing pinned, could not clear at all; (c) **kind coherence is enforced** on single triggers too; (d) reset leaves **inherit the trigger's measurement window** and have no window control of their own; (e) the two triggers a CONDITION is refused on get the **counterpart Event** instead (`TRIGGER_TYPES_WITH_RESET_EVENT`, the complement set) — `agent.disconnected` clears on `agent.connected`, scoped to the SAME subject the alert is about and prefilled from the server-published `RESET_EVENT_SUGGESTIONS` map, so an event automation is no longer stuck clearing itself on a timer whether or not anything recovered. A signal that writes ONE verb for both directions must be **split** before it can reset — a reset reads actionPattern and resourceType only, so it would otherwise match the rule's own escalation — and the recovery verb is written only on a landing at the healthy state, never on a partial recovery (`capacity.severity_recovered` and `platform.lifecycle_recovered`; both halves stay `resourceType: "system"` with no `resourceName` so the subject key matches). The trigger's own direction lives in `trigger.detailsMatch`, which the wizard's event step renders, collects and states in the trigger sentence — it was matched by the engine and shown by nothing until 2026-09-17, so opening one of those rules and saving it stripped the condition and the rule started firing both ways. And **`cooldownSec` was RETIRED from the builder** (2026-08) — it decided how often a NEW alert may fire, and had moved to the Actions step for that reason, but "Repeat this notification" answers the question operators were actually reaching for it to answer. The column and the engine's two checks survive, **dormant**, the `failureThreshold` precedent (business rule 36): the `clearNotificationCooldowns` one-shot nulls it fleet-wide (auditing each rule's old value FIRST, since the update destroys it), and the wizard writes null on every save, so a value an API caller re-sets cannot outlive the next edit in the builder. Removing the control without clearing the data would have left a rule silenced by a number no Polaris surface could show or edit. → [narrative-25-35.md#rule-32](narrative-25-35.md#rule-32)

33. **A device that answers is not a device that works — and the check that proves it belongs to the VENDOR, not to a login** — the HTTP check is a **manufacturer custom widget** (`ManufacturerCustomWidget.widgetType="http"`), not a polling method (retired 2026-08): a check varies by vendor AND model while a login varies by vendor, so sharing one row meant duplicating passwords. Decisions: the **body match is load-bearing** (a 200 whose body doesn't match FAILS; no lax toggle); **status is judged before content**; **redirects are never followed**; the body is **capped at 64 KB**; the credential is **authentication only, with no "none" mode**; **`authMode` is stated, not guessed** (Digest is a handshake — fire bare, read the challenge, re-fire **exactly once**); it writes `AssetStateSample` 0/1 **pass or fail** plus a success-only RTT gauge and **never moves `monitorStatus`**; and **`netGuard` deliberately does not apply**, the target being the monitored asset's own private address. → [narrative-25-35.md#rule-33](narrative-25-35.md#rule-33)

34. **An active scan finds things; a separate grant adds them** — a **Discovery** (`NetworkScan`) sweeps addresses the operator names, making it the only Polaris feature that touches hosts it has no prior relationship with, so the posture matters more than the mechanism. Seven decisions carry it: **scanning and adopting are separate grants** (`networkScan` authors and runs, `assets:write` chains on adopt, and the wizard RENDERS the missing grant rather than discovering it at the POST); it is **opt-in and IDS-visible**, with no scheduler and no shipped default range; **credential order is the try order** and the first method that answers wins; **adoption is new-addresses-only**, so a re-run enriches nothing and "nothing new" stays distinguishable from "nothing there"; it is deliberately **neither an 8th integration type nor a `DiscoveryRun` row**; and there is deliberately **no `network-scan` AssetSource kind**; and a Discovery is **private or shared**, where visibility decides who may see and RUN one while ownership decides who may edit it (`networkScan:fullwrite` reaching anyone's), an invisible row answering 404 rather than 403, `name` unique per owner, existing rows migrated public and new ones defaulting private, and the export carrying no visibility at all. → [narrative-25-35.md#rule-34](narrative-25-35.md#rule-34)

35. **The GAL is a mirror, not a source of truth — and Polaris only ever deletes what it wrote** — directory sync is opt-in per integration (`config.enableDirectorySync`, default off, deliberately separate from `enableDirectorySearch`, which still persists nothing) and runs as a post-sync pass of the owning integration's discovery run, never as its own scheduler job. Five invariants bound it: (a) **provenance decides deletion** — `DirectoryContactSource` records every (integration, directory object) the sync created, and a `Contact` dies only when its LAST provenance row goes; a hand-added row has none and is never touched, which is also why adoption drops provenance; (b) **a Polaris `User` or a manual `Contact` on an address wins** — the sync never creates over either, and an account created later retires the synced row on the next run; (c) **an empty or catastrophically-shrunken read never wipes** — zero entries, or a delete set over the guard, skips the deletion half and warns, because a revoked grant must not empty the address book; (d) **synced entries are gated on `automationManagement`, not `contacts:read`** — and so is the live GAL fan-out, since gating one and not the other leaves the roster reachable a query at a time; (e) **no directory PII reaches `Event.details` or the logs** — the run Event carries counts only, because Events are readable by anyone with events access and are shipped off-host by the syslog and SFTP archivers. → [narrative-25-35.md#rule-35](narrative-25-35.md#rule-35)

36. **The automation decides what "down" means; a device no automation covers is never judged** — a `monitorStatus == down` automation carries a **`missedPolls`** count, and that count IS the definition of down for every device it covers (`downDetectionService.resolveDownThreshold`, called once per probe); **it is the trigger's ONLY timing number** — the wizard states it in the trigger's own `Sustained for (polls)` field (there is no separate control, and none on the condition row) and zeroes `forPolls` / `forDurationSec` on a bare down trigger, because a hold beside the count would wait out the verdict the count itself declares and silently double the operator's number; precedence is rule 18's existing ladder — same-rank ties resolve to the **smaller count**, then older `createdAt`, then lower `id`. **A device covered by NO down automation reads `passive`**, the sixth `monitorStatus` value: still polled, still sampled, still charted — Polaris just renders no verdict. The counters keep advancing while passive; heavy cadences run for a passive asset whose last probe succeeded (`runsHeavyCadences`); entering/leaving passive is a **configuration edge** (`isConfigStatusEdge` — no `monitor.status_changed` Event, no dependency propagation); anything testing `!== "up"` as a proxy for "unreachable" must exempt it. A zero-rule fleet stamps a one-shot `monitor.down_detection_absent` warning Event. `failureThreshold` stays on the settings tiers as a dormant column read only by the V3 seed. **The same automation owns the way back UP** (2026-08-29): its reset hold — collected by the wizard in POLLS — holds the asset in `recovering` until that many probes have ANSWERED (`recoveryPollsFor`, which reads the STATED count `reset.sustainPolls` when there is one and falls back to dividing the `sustainSec` mirror by the asset's own cadence for a rule authored before counts were stored), so "down after 3 missed, reset after 5 received" describes the pill and not just the alert. **Since 2026-09-01 the DRAIN itself serves that count**: the bucket locks at `max(missedPolls, recoveryPolls)` on the verdict miss, so an outage owes exactly the operator's number of answers and no more — a four-minute outage and a four-day one cost the same climb. It was a FLOOR before, which meant a deep outage out-drained the reset and the number the operator wrote down stopped applying at exactly the outages it mattered most for. Two consequences: **`Asset.awaitingRecoveryConfirm` and `owesRecoveryConfirmation` are RETIRED** — the ambiguity that stored bit resolved (at `cf == 0`, a success run of N could be a drained bucket or a healthy device) cannot arise when the bucket carries the whole debt, so the column is dormant on the `cooldownSec` precedent and zeroed once by `jobs/clampFailureBucket.ts` — and the ENGINE must not charge the wait twice — `downRecoveryConsumesSustain` zeroes the engine-side sustain and makes `recoveredMeets` hold the alert through `down`/`recovering`/`warning` so it ends when the device reads `up` rather than on its first answered packet. Only `mode: "auto"` counts; a `condition` reset is its own recovery authority (rule 32a) and layering a poll count under it would be two clocks. **The automation also decides what colour Down is** (2026-09-01): `down` is painted in the covering automation's own `severity` on every surface — the Last-30-min strip, the desktop and phone response-time charts, and the chart rasterized into the alert email — through one palette duplicated by necessity in `downSeverityHex` (`utils/severityStyle.ts`, for the email) and `DOWN_SEV_COLOR` (`public/js/chart-severity.js`, for the browser), pinned to each other by `tests/unit/downSeverityColor.test.ts`. Red is what `critical` looks like, and critical is merely the default severity of a seeded down automation; an operator who rates an outage on a device class `warning` has said something about how it should read. `critical` keeps `#d32f2f` so a default install is unchanged, and an absent or unrecognized severity — passive devices and unresolved automations both — falls back to it rather than understating a verdict Polaris is still asserting. The palette is deliberately NOT `severityHex` (which colours a whole alert, where nothing competes): these land on charts already spending amber on a below-threshold miss, blue on a recovering probe and grey on a dependency-explained one, so each value is pulled deeper than the neighbour it sits beside. `warning` vs the miss amber is the closest pair and is a real limit rather than a bug — a warning-severity outage and the misses that built it are two shades of one yellow, separated by the tooltip and by the pill, which names the state outright. The **Status pill deliberately does NOT move**: its text already names the state, and recolouring it would make the Down and Missed badges the same colour in a list column where they are read as a set. → [narrative-36-43.md#rule-36](narrative-36-43.md#rule-36)

37. **An automation only fires about a device Polaris is actually polling** — ONE gate, `assetCanTrigger` in `notificationEngine`, applied by EVERY trigger path: the asset must be `monitored` **and** not suppressed (maintenance window / dependency-suppressed — business rule 16, which this rule now subsumes rather than sitting beside). The monitored half is pushed into SQL for the threshold, composite and metric/state-preview loads (`scopeWhere(scope, monitoredOnly)`) and checked per row in the event/change tail off the `assetDetail` row. **Event and change triggers were the deliberate exception and no longer are**: firing about a device nobody polls made "an automation covers it" and "Polaris watches it" two different answers. The four statuses that cannot carry monitoring at all fold in through business rule 10 rather than being retested here. Three consequences: un-monitoring a device **clears** its live alerts through `clearVanishedStates` (`notification.out_of_scope`) the way leaving the scope does — which is the bug that forced the rule, since `monitorStatus` is NOT cleared when monitoring stops and a `monitorStatus == down` automation kept firing forever about a shelved device; a **null `assetDetail` still fires** (the asset row is GONE — an `asset.deleted` event must not be swallowed), as do system-scoped events, which carry no assetId; and the raw unfiltered scope resolver stays for the surfaces asking a different question — the device-list preview REPORTS the unmonitored remainder, and the dimension picker, mass pinning and `Contact.assetCondition` are not about firing. → [narrative-36-43.md#rule-37](narrative-12-24.md#rule-3

38. **A device behind a dark parent is not accused, and it is not released on one packet** — two halves of dependency suppression that had been left implicit. (a) **Release is asymmetric.** Entering suppression still needs every effective parent confirmed `down` (`warning`/`recovering` flapping must never drag a healthy subtree in), but LEAVING it needs the parent genuinely BACK: only `up` releases, and every state short of it (`down`, `recovering`, `warning`) holds — a parent mid-recovery has answered ONE packet and is still short of the covering automation's success threshold, so releasing there un-suppresses a subtree whose own probes are all still failing and the outage re-alerts device by device as plain Down. The child's persisted `dependencySuppressed` selects the side (`SuppressionAssetState.currentlySuppressed`); the release side is a WHITELIST (`RELEASES_SUPPRESSION` — `up`, `unknown`, `passive`) so a state added later cannot leak a subtree by default. `unknown` and `passive` are in it because neither is a claim about reachability and gating release on them would strand a subtree in Dep. Down with nothing able to clear it. `warning` sat with them on that same argument until 2026-09-14, and the argument never applied to it: it is the one state in which the parent has just MISSED a poll — the least plausible moment to call it recovered — and it can strand nothing, being transient by construction (keep missing and the bucket reaches the threshold; keep answering and it drains to `up`). What it leaked was real: a parent flapping `down` → `recovering` → `recovering` → `warning` (a miss taken once the bucket has drained below threshold-1, per `nextFailureBucket`) put its whole subtree back on the air mid-outage one reconciler tick later, every child re-alerting as plain Down — the storm the rule exists to prevent, reached by the one door left open. Release cascades one layer per pass, which is correct — a gate coming back does not mean the switch under it has. (b) **A miss the upstream explains is GREY, not red.** A suppressed asset is still probed (half cadence — it may answer over a redundant path), and every failure used to be indistinguishable from a real one. `recordProbeResult` now stamps `AssetMonitorSample.dependencyDown` from the asset row's own flag (never re-derived — the reconciler owns it), the rollups carry `dependencyFailureCount`, and `probeOutageService` hands every chart an `OutageKind`. Same dive, no accusation; deliberately NOT a band (that vocabulary is maintenance's, rule 16). A run **splits at a change of kind**, a rollup bucket must qualify WHOLE (`dependencyFailureCount >= failureCount`; NULL reads as red), and the grey is duplicated in four places by design like the red already is. → [narrative-36-43.md#rule-38](narrative-36-43.md#rule-38)

39. **How a person wants to be reached is theirs, not the automation's — but an automation offering one way still reaches them** — `User.notificationPreference` (`email` default | `push` | `any`) is an ACCOUNT setting, not a browser switch: the "Enable push" toggle is gone from the account menu, the mobile More tab and the Automations page, and every client instead reconciles ITS OWN subscription to the stored preference at boot (`polarisPush.syncToPreference`), which is what makes "prefer push" mean push on every device. That reconcile **never prompts** (no user activation at page load), so a browser that has never been asked cannot be enrolled by it; instead that browser is **asked once** — `polarisPush.shouldOfferEnrollment` gates a one-time dialog (desktop, `_openPushOfferDialog` off the `wireNotificationPrefs` boot chain) or bottom sheet (mobile, `openPushOfferSheet` off `initPushOnce`) whose Enable click is the activation the permission prompt needs. It offers only at permission **`default`** (`granted` is already the reconcile's, `denied` cannot be re-prompted from script), only when the account prefers push, the browser holds no subscription and the SERVER has Web Push configured, and **never on iOS outside the installed home-screen app**. The offer records itself in `localStorage` the moment it is SHOWN, not when a button is clicked — the scrim, the X and Escape hand back no callback, and a dialog that reopens until answered one specific way is a nag — so it is asked once per ACCOUNT per BROWSER and the account menu's Notifications row is the way back. That record is a browser fact and never an account one. The preference itself is **saved even when the browser refuses**, being account-wide. A notify action is now **plural** — `channelIds` carries several channels behind one recipient list, `channelId` survives as the primary and the lossless single-channel mirror (`notifyChannelIds` is the ONLY reader of the pair; `assertActionRefs` refuses a payload where the two disagree), and `actionsToTargets` expands one action to one DeliveryTarget per channel so nothing downstream knows an action can be plural. On top of that, `respectUserPreference` delivers to each recipient only through the channel their own preference accepts — consulted **ONLY when the action group offers BOTH methods** (`actionsCarryBothMethods` server-side, `hostOffersBothMethods` in the wizard, which grays the checkbox), since in a single-method group the preference would DELETE the alert rather than route it. Only ACCOUNTS are filtered (a typed address or an address-book contact expressed no preference — unknown means deliver, the rule 25 posture), and **a preference never deletes an alert**: `preferenceWithholds` keeps a recipient on the channel they didn't pick when the one they did pick can't reach them (no enrolled device, or a null `User.email`) — reachability meaning the SUBSCRIPTION EXISTS, since a 201 from a push service is not delivery and a dead endpoint only surfaces at send time, where `enqueuePushFallbacks` re-routes it to the action's own email channel behind three guards (per-recipient not per-row, the action's own channel, never twice) — worth exactly one alert per dead endpoint, since the 410 also prunes the subscription and every later alert takes the fire-time path, an unrecognized preference normalizes to `email` rather than to nobody, and the two broadcast toggles stayed push-only (applied on `transport === "web_push"` alone, so a mixed action never mails the whole roster off a control that never said so). Changes write `user.notification_preference.changed`. → [narrative-36-43.md#rule-39](narrative-36-43.md#rule-39)

40. **Two assets on one address is a conflict; one asset on a stale address is not** — `Asset.ipAddress` carries no uniqueness constraint (two rows holding one address is the state discovery has to be able to REPRESENT in order to report it, unlike the IPAM half's per-subnet unique index — rule 3), so the duplicate is found by a sweep (`detectDuplicateIpAssets`, 10m) and reported as the `duplicate-ip` Conflict flavour + a `conflict.detected` Event, which is what the baseline "IP conflict detected" automation alerts on. Seven decisions carry it. (a) **Only network-present assets count** — status NOT in `UNMONITORABLE_STATUSES` (business rule 10), i.e. `active` + `maintenance`; a shelved, disabled or decommissioned row is not on the wire. (b) **A stale record is not a duplicate.** DHCP reuses addresses constantly and an Asset keeps the last address a writer staged until another write moves it, so a departed device's row sits on an address its successor legitimately holds — reporting those would bury the real ones. A member counts only while its claim is CURRENT: operator-owned (`ipOverride` equals the address, or `ipSource="manual"`) never expires, and a discovered one must have been re-asserted within `CLAIM_FRESH_DAYS` (7) — measured on the `(asset, ip)` `AssetIpHistory` row, which the `db.ts` extension bumps on EVERY write staging `ipAddress` (so it tracks cadence, not change), with `Asset.lastSeen` as the fallback for a row that predates the extension. The per-address timestamp is preferred over the asset's presence on purpose: a device that is up but whose recorded address nobody has re-asserted in months is a stale RECORD. (c) **It takes two DEVICES, not two rows** — members sharing one non-null MAC are one device recorded twice, which is a merge's problem (`mergeDuplicateHostnameAssets`), not an address conflict; a null MAC proves nothing, so each counts as its own device. (d) **One pending row per ADDRESS, never per pair** — a three-way collision is one card carrying every claimant in `proposedAssetFields.members[]`, and the row's `assetId` is the lowest-id member so a refresh can't churn the FK its cascade rides on. (e) **Accept is refused, because there is nothing to adopt — there are TWO verbs instead, one per real cause.** Two devices: `POST /conflicts/:id/reassign-ip`, a new address typed on ONE member, written with the asset form's own pin semantics (`ipAddress` + `ipOverride` + `ipSource="manual"` in one update, so the override guard defers and discovery converging on it later releases the pin by itself) — and it **refuses an address another network-present asset already holds**, which would move the duplicate rather than resolve it. One device recorded twice: `POST /conflicts/:id/merge` with `{ survivorAssetId, absorbAssetIds }`, which absorbs the named members into the survivor through `assetMergeService.mergeAssets` — the same engine the asset page's Merge modal uses, so provenance/MACs/IP history/dependency edges/monitoring carry identically — accepting **no field winners** (blank-fill is what every automatic absorb uses; per-field control lives on the asset's Sources tab) and re-pointing `Conflict.assetId` at the survivor BEFORE the first merge, since deleting an absorbed asset cascades to conflicts pointing at it. Reject means dismiss, and the rejected row is the dedup marker: the SAME member set never re-raises, a changed one does. (f) **A duplicate that resolves itself closes itself**, as `rejected` by `system:auto-resolved` — nothing may leave a resolved collision sitting in the queue; that same path is what retires a conflict whose members stop qualifying, so narrowing (g) needs no migration. (g) **One claimant has to be equipment somebody ADDRESSED on purpose** — `CONFLICT_ELIGIBLE_ASSET_TYPES` (`switch` / `access_point` / `firewall` / `server`, the last covering vCenter VMs, which are typed `server`). Two endpoints trading a pool address is DHCP working, not a fault; an endpoint sitting on an access point's address is an outage. So ONE qualifying member raises the group and every other claimant still rides the card (the endpoint is what took the address, and the operator usually resolves it by moving the endpoint). Eligibility is tested on the CURRENT claims, so a departed switch's leftover record can't license a conflict between two live laptops. It is a constant, not a Setting — a claim about which equipment has deliberate addressing rather than a per-install preference — and `hypervisor` (an ESXi host) and `router` are deliberately outside it pending a call on whether they belong. (h) **OR the ADDRESS was assigned on purpose and the claimants came from different integrations** — the cross-source clause, a second, independent way past (g)'s bar, because (g) asks whether the DEVICE has deliberate addressing and this asks whether the ADDRESS does. Both halves are required. `groupHasDisjointSources`: two claimants whose `AssetSource.sourceKind` sets share NOTHING (the AD/Entra/Intune row carries `{ad, entra, intune}`, the FortiGate row carries `{fortigate-endpoint}`, and nothing cross-linked them) — a shared kind means one integration reported both rows, which is that integration's identity resolution to fix, and a member with NO source rows abstains rather than qualifying, since unknown provenance is not evidence that two integrations disagree. `addressIsDeliberate`: an active `Reservation` on the CONTAINING subnet (through `buildIpContexts`, so a reservation in an unrelated overlapping block cannot license anything) where `vipInfo` is non-null, `dhcpBinding = "reservation"`, or `sourceType` ∈ `DELIBERATE_RESERVATION_SOURCE_TYPES` (`manual` / `dhcp_reservation` / `interface_ip` / `vip`). **All three columns are read because rule 23 makes them three separate facts** — discovery Phase 3c stamps `vipInfo` on a row still labelled `dhcp_lease`, and Phase 5 writes `dhcpBinding` without ever flipping `sourceType`, so `sourceType` alone is wrong in both directions. A plain `dhcp_lease` is deliberately NOT deliberate: on a leased address two genuinely different devices may hold it days apart inside `CLAIM_FRESH_DAYS`, and reporting those would bury the real ones — the same reasoning that gave (g) its list. What (h) reports is overwhelmingly ONE device recorded twice, so the card leads with the merge rather than with renumbering. `DuplicateIpGroup.qualifiedBy` (`"asset-type"` | `"cross-source"`) records which clause admitted the group and rides onto `proposedAssetFields.qualifiedBy`; (g) is tested first and keeps the label when both apply, and a conflict raised before this clause shipped carries no value, which the card reads as `"asset-type"`. The inputs are resolved once per pass by `loadCrossSourceContext`, which EVERY caller of `groupCurrentClaims` that can raise or close must pass — omitting it on the single-address refresh path would auto-close the conflict the scan had just raised, every tick. (i) **OR an operator TYPED the address** (2026-09-22) — a third way past (g)'s bar: (g) asks whether the DEVICE has deliberate addressing, (h) whether the ADDRESS does, and this asks whether a PERSON did the addressing. A claim that is operator-owned (`claimIsOperatorOwned` — `ipSource="manual"` or a pin equal to the address) is an address somebody chose exactly as a reservation chooses one, so two workstations one of which was hand-addressed is a collision worth a card. Narrowest and newest: it takes the `qualifiedBy` label (`"operator-addressed"`) only when neither older clause applies, and the scan's SQL prefilter admits it as a third `bool_or` superset. It is what makes the WRITE-TIME check mean anything, because without it the sweep would auto-close the card a save had just raised. **The sweep is no longer the only trigger.** The two operator writes that put a device on an address — `POST /assets` and a `PUT /assets/:id` that CHANGES `ipAddress` — run the same reconcile SCOPED to the addresses they touched (`reconcileDuplicateIpForAddresses`: the new address, and on a move the old one too, so a collision the move just ended closes now rather than in ten minutes) and return the card as `ipConflict`; a `POST /assets/:id/merge` re-evaluates the survivor's address fire-and-forget. Scoping is what keeps this honest — a scoped pass neither reads nor closes pending conflicts on addresses it did not look at. Imports are deliberately NOT triggers: `POST /assets/import` writes no address and `/import-pdf` creates assets as `storage`, which (a) excludes. **Before the write, the form asks** (`GET /assets/ip-check`): `checkIpForIncomingClaim` SIMULATES the incoming claim as a synthetic operator-owned row grouped with the real claims through the SAME `groupCurrentClaims`, so what the dialog warns about is exactly what the save raises; a failed check never blocks a save (the sweep is the backstop). The dialog offers "save & submit for conflict review" to everyone and "save & review merge" only when the server says `canMerge`, computed as `assets:fullwrite`. **Merging takes `assets:fullwrite`** — it is editing one asset AND deleting another, the destructive tier the key already uses for agent deploy — on `POST /assets/:id/merge`, the conflict verb's chained check and the merge modal alike, read off the matrix rather than a role name (until this the UI opened it admin-only while the API accepted any `assets:write`); the reassign verb stays `write` because it deletes nothing. → [narrative-36-43.md#rule-40](narrative-36-43.md#rule-40)

41. **A subnet dies with its FortiGate, and the chassis — not the name — says which gate that is** — a discovered `Subnet` records the SERIAL of the gate serving it (`Subnet.fortigateSerial`) alongside `fortigateDevice`'s name, because a name cannot tell a RENAME from a REPLACEMENT and those need opposite handling. **Tri-state in both directions** (`utils/chassisIdentity.ts:classifyChassis`): a NULL stored serial is unknown and LEARNS on first sight (so the migration backfills nothing — every existing row converges on its own), and an unreadable serial THIS run is unknown and applies no constraint, never "different", or one failed CMDB read would declare the whole fleet replaced. **The comparison is against a SET, per device** — the reporting gate's own serial plus every `ha_slave[]` member — because FMG flips a cluster's top-level `sn` to whichever member is active and comparing against one value would report a replacement on every failover; per DEVICE and not fleet-wide, since a chassis re-registered under another FMG device entry is genuinely a replacement for THIS subnet. Anything outside that set raises the `chassis-replaced` Conflict (`subnetChassisConflictService`, the first `entityType="subnet"` variant), which is **additive**: the old chassis's subnet + reservations are COPIED to the archive, nothing is released, re-pushed or deleted, and the stored serial is deliberately NOT re-pointed while the conflict is pending (the pending row IS the unresolved state; accepting moves it). Dedup is keyed on the (old, new) serial PAIR, so a rejected row suppresses exactly that transition while a second swap raises anew. This closes a case that was previously SILENT: a same-name RMA swap matched by CIDR, matched the roster, and let the new chassis inherit every reservation row of the old one, `pushStatus: "synced"` and dead device-side pointers included. **Discovery RETIRES a dead row itself**: Phase 1's create path consults a second index of the deprecated rows (`deprecatedByBlockCidr`, keyed exactly as the unique index is) and archives one when a live gate re-reports its range — but ONLY when a DIFFERENT gate is serving it (`classifyDeprecatedSupersede`), since an operator who deprecated a subnet its own gate still serves would otherwise have that decision silently undone. Serial decides where both sides have one; the device NAME is the fallback for rows predating the column, and a matching name with no stored serial is genuinely ambiguous and KEPT (a wrongly-kept row is a skipped subnet someone can archive by hand; a wrongly-archived one reactivates a retired range). Exact-CIDR only — a deprecated /23 partially overlapping a new /24 is a different judgement and is still reported as a real overlap. The remaining skip NAMES the fix rather than emitting the old self-overlap message, and the rule 42 excluded-address check runs BEFORE it so an out-of-scope CIDR never reaches archiving. This was the feature's blind spot on release: the chassis comparison sits inside the `if (existing)` branch and `existing` excludes deprecated rows, so on an install whose gate was already swapped it never ran at all and the operator saw the identical skipped-subnet event on the fixed build. **And a retired subnet MOVES to `ArchivedSubnet` / `ArchivedReservation` rather than going `deprecated`** — a deprecated row still holds `@@unique([blockId, cidr])` while being invisible to discovery's lookup index, so its CIDR became unrecordable rather than reusable (every run skipped it with a self-overlap message, and `findSubnetForIp` dropped every lease, DHCP reservation, VIP and interface IP inside it too); moving the row out is the only thing that frees the index, and it makes the retired rows **locked by construction** rather than by a guard at each of ~50 reservation write sites. Archiving is deliberately exempt from rule 4's active-reservation protection (that guards against destruction; this preserves). Rule 23 is unchanged — a decommissioned managed device still gives its address back, while the subnet is live. A second, independent defect fixed in the same change: Phase 1 re-pointed a matched subnet's `fortigateDevice` in the DB but never mirrored it onto the in-memory row Phase 2 filters, so a subnet re-pointed at a renamed or replaced gate was deprecated by Phase 2 **in the same run** — a plain gate rename was enough. → [narrative-36-43.md#rule-41](narrative-36-43.md#rule-41)

42. **Some address space is not one network, and the way to say so is to exclude it** — a `SubnetExclusion` is a CIDR the operator has declared out of scope for the networks list, managed on IPAM → Networks → **Exclusions**. It exists because a Polaris subnet is ONE row per CIDR while some address space is the SAME at every site (a management VLAN, an out-of-band range, an appliance's fixed subnet): the first site discovered claims the row, every other site collides with it, and since rule 41 that collision is a CONFLICT rather than noise — each site's gate answers with its own serial, so the shared row reads `replaced` on every run and raises a `chassis-replaced` card about a box nobody swapped, with a fresh (old, new) pair each time so dedup never catches it. **Global, not per block** (the CIDR needs excluding precisely because several sites serve it; a per-block exclusion would let it in through another block). **`cidr` is the IDENTITY** — normalized on write and FROZEN after create (the PUT and its Zod schema take `name`/`notes` only), because re-pointing an exclusion in place would un-exclude what the operator excluded and exclude what they never named, in one edit; changing the range is a delete plus an add. **Containment is ONE-directional**: an exclusion covers itself and anything narrower (so a /16 excludes the /24s in it) and deliberately NOT a wider discovered CIDR, or excluding one /24 would silently swallow a /8; the MOST SPECIFIC match is reported so a refusal names the row to delete. **One enforcement seam for creation** — `createSubnetRowChecked`, for the same reason rule 20a's overlap lock lives there: manual create, auto-allocate, bulk allocate and the discovery create all pass through it, and `opts.exclusions` lets a caller that already loaded the set (discovery, once per run) pay no read per row. **The ALLOCATORS treat excluded ranges as taken space, never as a refusal** — folded into the list handed to `findNextAvailableSubnet` / `packIntoAnchor`, so "any free /N" steps over an exclusion instead of 409-ing on it, and `previewBulkAllocate` must build that list identically or the preview shows a plan the write rejects. **Discovery skips a covered scope WHOLE** (Phase 1, ABOVE the existing-row branch — the conflict comes from the UPDATE side, so skipping only the create would fix nothing) and exempts the row from the **Phase 2** stale sweep, since its `fortigateDevice` is frozen at whichever site claimed it first and deprecating it when that one gate leaves the roster would put it in exactly the `deprecated` state rule 41 exists to avoid. **Adding an exclusion destroys NOTHING** — networks already listed that it covers come back as `matchCount`/`matches` and are left in place (retiring one stays `POST /subnets/:id/archive`, the verb that preserves reservations); a config write must not delete address-space records as a side effect, and the count is reported rather than hidden so "nothing happened" and "something happened I wasn't told about" don't look alike. IPv4-only, refused at the door rather than stored as a row that could never match. Reads `subnets:read`, mutations `subnets:**fullwrite**` (the archive's reasoning — fleet-wide, over discovered rows whose `createdBy` is null). → [narrative-36-43.md#rule-42](narrative-36-43.md#rule-42)

43. **A grant is only as narrow as the act it names** — three permission levels described acts they did not match, and all three move the same way. (a) **Deploying the Polaris Agent is `assets=fullwrite`, not `write`**: install / retry / reinstall / upgrade / uninstall + `POST /assets/bulk-agent-install` all run an installer on someone else's host over a stored SSH/WinRM credential and leave a service behind, which is not the act `assets=write` (fix a hostname, retype a serial) describes — `assetsadmin` got fleet deployment free with the inventory job. Enabling an integration class block's **`agentDeploy.enabled` is the SAME grant, CHAINED** onto `integrations=write` (`assertAgentDeployGrant` in `api/routes/integrations.ts`), because a capability with two doors has to be gated at both: `networkadmin` holds `assets=read` and could not deploy to one device, but reached the fleet-wide checkbox through a route that never mentions assets. Only the ON transition is gated — a role that inherits an enabled block must still be able to switch it off. Reads stay at `read`; the client withholds the verbs through `canDeployAgent()` / `data-deploy-agent`, never `canManageAssets()`. (b) **`assetsProbe` is a READ-ONLY key** (`levels: ["none","read"]`): it was gated at `write` everywhere, so `read` granted nothing and `fullwrite` was asked for by no route — two dead radio buttons an admin could still click. The one thing on the key that WROTE, the dependency-test outage simulation (it stamps `Asset.dependencyTestUntil` and can mask a real outage), moved to **`assetMonitorSettings=fullwrite`** — the admin-only level its own code comment always claimed and the `assetsProbe=write` gate never delivered. (c) **`credentials` carries the ownership dimension**: `write` creates + reaches own rows, `fullwrite` reaches any, `POST /credentials/test` included whenever it names a stored `id` (that path merges the row's REAL secrets into the probe, so it lends a password rather than reading anything), and an unowned row — `createdBy` null, never backfilled — is fullwrite-only. Ladder narrowing is enforced by `normalizePermissions` (clamps DOWN, never up), `permissionOf` (clamps on read, for pre-deploy session snapshots) and `requirePermission` (throws at module load on an ungrantable level). (d) **Extended catalogue-wide 2026-09-22** (migration `20260922000000_rbac_catalogue_hygiene`): (b) was not one key — **a rung exists only where it grants something the rung below it does not**. Seventeen keys carried one that nothing asked for and `processControl` gated nothing at all, so it was removed; fourteen keys lost `fullwrite`, four (`serverSettingsData`, `maintenanceManagement`, `automationManagement`, `automationScripts`) had skipped `write` and had their routes moved DOWN onto it, and `fullwrite` now survives only on an ownership key or one of seven named acts — pinned by `tests/unit/permissionLevelLadders.test.ts` → "no dead top rung", which fails the build on a key offering it without a reason. Two levels moved because they misdescribed the act, exactly as (a) did: **downloading a backup is the whole database leaving the host, not a read**, so it is `serverSettingsData=write` and that key now has no `read` rung (its other reads ride the mount's `serverSettingsSystem=read` floor, making it the only `none | write` ladder); and the **asset auto-decommission thresholds** moved off `events` — the audit-log key — onto `assetMonitorSettings`, which its only UI already rides. Knowingly left wrong and flagged in that test: `serverSettingsSystem=write` gates ONLY the identity providers while `fullwrite` gates the other ~54 routes, so repointing every login sits a rung below changing the logo — the fix is an `authentication` key, not a shorter ladder. **Second invariant from the same sweep: `readonly` is the FLOOR for every built-in role** — nothing that exists to do more than look may reach less than the look-only role, with ONE deliberate exception (`user` holds `networkScan=none` where `readonly` holds `read`; that role is for address-space self-service and a sweep is IDS-visible). No capability was taken away: every stored `fullwrite` on a shortened key folds to that key's surviving top rung. → [narrative-36-43.md#rule-43](narrative-36-43.md#rule-43)

44. **A quiet window withholds the reminder, not the alert — and the reminder that follows says how long** — an automation's `repeat.quiet` is a list of up to 8 recurring windows (`utils/quietTime.ts`, validated by the Maintenance scheduler's own `scheduleShapeSchema` and evaluated by its `currentWindow`/`nextWindow`, so every time is SERVER-LOCAL wall clock and a 22:00–06:00 window survives DST and midnight identically to a maintenance window). Since a day carries a LIST of hour ranges and each day may keep its own, ONE window says "nights during the week, all weekend" — which is why the wizard edits a single window with the shared `PolarisRecurrence` day/hours editor and lists any others (a one-shot, a monthly freeze) read-only rather than offering to rewrite them. Five things it is, and one it is not. (a) **Held, never skipped**: the sweep does not advance `escalationState.tiers.repeat.lastSentAt` on a reminder it withholds, so that reminder stays DUE and goes out on the first tick after the window ends — there is nothing to schedule, a held reminder is simply an overdue one. (b) **The reminder that ends a hold reports the silence**: the hold is stamped on `escalationState.quietHeldSince` / `quietHeldCount`, and that stamp puts `{repeat.quiet}` ("Reminders resumed after a quiet period — this alert has been active for 9h 12m.") in the body and `· ACTIVE 9h 12m` in the `[REMINDER n]` subject, because the question after a silent night is how long this has been going on, not which reminder number arrived. The subject marker rides ONLY that reminder — on every reminder it would mean nothing — and only when no operator subject template is set, the same condition the `[REMINDER n]` prefix already carried. The standing `Active for` facts row is separate and rides EVERY reminder (it renders "" and prunes away on the initial alert). (c) **The hold is closed by the SEND, not by the window ending**: a reminder whose channel was dead retries next sweep and must still be the one that reports the silence. (d) **A hold only exists where a reminder was actually withheld** — quiet with nothing due stamps nothing, so a reminder that comes due 20 minutes after the window ended is an ordinary reminder and says so. (e) **A malformed quiet blob must not silence the reminders it was only supposed to pause**: `normalizeRuleToV2` re-parses the repeat config with `quiet` stripped rather than reading the whole thing as "never repeats", which would have turned "pause overnight" into "never remind anyone again" — invisible until someone missed an outage. What it is NOT: quiet time does not touch the first alert, the escalation tiers, or the reset notifications. A tier exists to chase a specific person harder, so silencing it from a control that says "reminders" would weaken an escalation the operator configured somewhere they weren't looking; `escalation.stopOn` and `repeat.stopOn` are still the only things that stop either. `stopAfterHours` is wall time from the fire, quiet included (the wizard warns about the pairing rather than extending the deadline), and the maintenance / dependency suppression pause is NOT a quiet hold — it `continue`s before the repeat pass and retires the alert outright (business rule 16). → [narrative-44-48.md#rule-44](narrative-44-48.md#rule-44)

45. **An address places a device only through the gate that owns it, and only a device nothing else can place** — for an asset with an IP address and NO MAC, `lastSeenSwitch` / `lastSeenAp` are derived by the `resolveIpUpstreamChain` sweep (`services/ipUpstreamChainService.ts`, boot + 90s then every 10 min, scheduler role) along ONE chain: IP → containing subnet's owning FortiGate (`Subnet.fortigateSerial` first, `fortigateDevice` second, through `utils/fortinetParentKey.ts` — never a hostname match) → THAT gate's `AssetArpEntry` → MAC → the `AssetMacTableEntry` learned port with the fewest MACs (`<hostname>/<ifName>`) and the `AssetWirelessStation` carrying the MAC or the address. **Four refusals:** another gate's ARP row never counts, and with no owning gate resolvable the address is accepted only when exactly ONE gate reports it (the overlapping-RFC1918 scoping of rules 17 and 26); two MACs at the address on the owning gate is `ambiguous`, not a pick (rule 26's ARP rule); an address claim that is not current under rule 40's model (`claimIsOperatorOwned` never expires, a discovered claim needs its `AssetIpHistory` row within `CLAIM_FRESH_DAYS`) is skipped; and ARP / FDB / station rows older than `EVIDENCE_FRESH_MS` (24h) are not evidence, because those tables are delete-replaced per scrape and an older row means the writer stopped answering. **MAC-less assets ONLY** (`macAddress IS NULL`, infra types excluded): a MAC-bearing asset already has Phase 7.5 and the FortiAP station scrape as writers, with a different label format, and a second writer would ping-pong the column and audit both halves every tick — the sweep writes only what no other writer can. **The derived MAC IS adopted onto the asset** (2026-09; it was withheld until then because adoption makes the row eligible for MAC-keyed dedupe and merge, and a wrong adoption merges two devices and deletes one row's monitoring history). What keeps it safe is `partitionAdoptableMacs`, not a Setting: the sweep's own refusals already rule out the ambiguous wire cases, so the only duplicate adoption can still manufacture is a MAC that ALREADY names a device — refused when another asset carries it, and refused for BOTH when two candidates in one pass resolve to the same one. A refusal drops the adoption only; the switch/AP stamps derived from the same MAC still apply, because those are reversible and a merge is not. Written in the same `$transaction` as the stamps, audited as `asset.mac.adopted`, and mirrored into `AssetMacAddress` with source `ip-upstream-arp` after the commit. Adoption is also what ENDS eligibility — the candidate query is `macAddress IS NULL`, so a placed row leaves the candidate set instead of being re-derived every tick. **Stamps are never cleared** — absence of evidence is not a move — and are written only on change, id-sorted inside `$transaction` chunks under `retryOnDeadlock`, with `asset.switch_port.changed` / `asset.wireless_ap.changed` Events (actor `system:upstream-chain`) batched AFTER each commit. Set-based end to end; no per-asset awaits. The Add Asset form's `/ip-context` panel gained the same station lookup (`apStations[]`, `matchedBy: "mac" | "ip"`). → [narrative-44-48.md#rule-45](narrative-44-48.md#rule-45)

46. **A device filter on an event automation filters the event's SUBJECT** — an `event` or `change` automation's `scope` selects which devices it fires ABOUT, and the event tail (`runEventTail` in `notificationEngine.ts`) enforces it: for a filtered rule, the Event's asset must be selected by `scopeMatchesAsset` or the fire is skipped. The test is read BEFORE the fired-this-event stamp, not with the state gates — an automation whose filter does not name the device is not watching that event at all, so it must not suppress its own reset either. Two refusals follow from "the subject is a device": an event that names no asset — an integration, a user, a login, the host itself — cannot satisfy a device filter, so a FILTERED automation never fires on one (an unconstrained one still does, which is how the twelve seeded event automations keep working); and neither does an event whose asset row is already gone (`asset.deleted`), because there is nothing left to test the filter against. **"Unconstrained" is its own question, asked first** — `scopeIsUnconstrained()` in `notificationTypes.ts`: `{allAssets:true}`, a bare `{}`, or a condition tree with no rules in it. It cannot be folded into `scopeMatchesAsset`, which answers false for `{}` (a builder-authored scope with no dimensions selects nothing) — the right answer for the builder and exactly the wrong one for every event automation saved before 2026-09, all of which carry `{}` meaning "any device". The trigger type is `scoped: true` in `buildSchemaCatalog` so the wizard SAVES the Devices step instead of discarding it, and `previewRule` answers a filtered event draft with the device list its filter selects (plus the note that the list is who it would fire about, not who is triggering now). `ASSET_SCOPED_TRIGGER_TYPES` still excludes `event` on purpose: that list answers "is this automation about a device", which is what the asset-details Alerts tab asks, and an all-assets `integration.discover.error` automation is about an integration, not about each of 2000 devices. Carve-out precedence is untouched — `triggerSignature` returns null for event and change (rule 18), so a filtered event automation neither carves out nor is carved out. → [narrative-44-48.md#rule-46](narrative-44-48.md#rule-46)
47. **A PostgreSQL client is chosen by the server's MAJOR and verified with `--version` before it is trusted — never spawned by bare name.** `pg_dump` refuses a server newer than itself, so `backupService.resolvePgTool()` reads `SHOW server_version_num`, looks for `/usr/pgsql-<N>/bin`, `/usr/lib/postgresql/<N>/bin` or the Windows install dir for that major (a NEWER major is accepted; an older one never), falls back to PATH only when nothing versioned exists — and when the server's major cannot be read at all, takes the NEWEST versioned client before PATH (the shell twin first borrows each versioned `psql` to ask the server; the host most likely to have no `psql` on PATH is the one that just removed AppStream 13 without `alternatives --auto`, 2026-09-10) — then runs `--version` on whatever it picked and compares majors (`src/utils/pgClientTools.ts`). A `pg_dump` older than the server refuses the backup with the sentence the operator needs — both versions, the path, the fix — as the AppError itself, never behind "see the server log"; `psql` only warns across majors, so for it only "cannot be run" is fatal. `deploy/update-linux.sh` carries the same logic in shell (`resolve_pg_tool`, `$PG_DUMP`, `$PSQL`); `setup-rhel-nodb.sh` installs PGDG's versioned client instead of RHEL's unversioned `postgresql` (which is PostgreSQL 13 on RHEL 9); both RHEL installers and both nodb installers compare the client major to the server's instead of asking `command -v` whether a file exists; `check:versions` flags a bare `dnf install -y postgresql`; CI's `integration` job installs `postgresql-client-17` by name rather than using the runner image's own client, and both that major and its `postgres:17-alpine` service container are `postgres-major` sites so they cannot drift apart (2026-09-10); the Maintenance tab shows the resolved tools and their compatibility. Presence is not compatibility, `alternatives --display` reports its own bookkeeping rather than the file on disk, and the Platform Lifecycle card grades the server — none of them can see this. → [narrative-44-48.md#rule-47](narrative-44-48.md#rule-47)

48. **Nobody hands out authority they do not hold** — admin-equivalence is `users=fullwrite AND roles=fullwrite` (`isAdminEquivalentPermissions`), and a caller who lacks it may not create it, on a role or on a user. `assertNoPrivilegeEscalation(req, targetPermissions, subject)` in `api/middleware/permissions.js` refuses with a **403** at the four places the grant could be minted: `POST /roles` and `PUT /roles/:id` (a `roles:write` holder writing the two fullwrite grants — onto a new role, or onto the role they already hold), and `POST /users` and `PUT /users/:id/role` (a `users:write` holder creating an account on an admin-equivalent role **with a password they choose**, or promoting an existing one into it). Before this, the write/fullwrite distinction on those two keys meant nothing: either delegated permission was a one-step path to full control of the install, and the shortest one needed no existing account at all. **It is not four-eyes** — a caller who already holds admin-equivalence is unaffected, and the check is skipped entirely whenever the target permission set is not admin-equivalent, so every ordinary role edit is untouched. **It is the mirror of the lastAdminEquivalent guard, and neither replaces the other**: that one refuses to DEMOTE the last admin (rule 43's neighbourhood, enforced by `countAdminEquivalentUsers`), this one refuses to PROMOTE into the tier; the self-edit checks on `PUT /users/:id/role` ("you cannot change your own role") were never escalation guards at all, since the escalation runs through somebody else's row. **The caller's own level is read from the request's role snapshot** — `req.roleSnapshot` for a bearer token, `req.session.roleSnapshot` otherwise — so a role-bound API token is held to the same bar as a session, and a request with no snapshot resolved is treated as NOT admin (it must never fail open). The predicate is shared with `rankRole`'s group-mapping tie-break so the notion cannot drift between "which role wins an SSO mapping" and "who may grant it". Server-side provisioning is deliberately outside the rule: `ssoProvisioning` and the seed have no escalating actor to test. → [narrative-44-48.md#rule-48](narrative-44-48.md#rule-48)

49. **An upgrade never refuses for want of a credential it can find itself, and never records one it has not proved** — `resolveUpgradeCredential` in `services/agentInstallService.ts` answers what an agent upgrade connects with, in one order: the operator's explicit `credentialId` if the request carried one, else the row's `installCredentialId`, else the Polaris-managed SSH deployment credential for the platform (`windowsSshOnboardingService.getOnboardingState`; darwin takes the Linux one). **An id that no longer resolves counts as absent, not as an error** — `ManagedAgent.installCredentialId` is written ONLY at install time and its FK is `ON DELETE SET NULL`, so deleting a credential silently strands every agent installed with it, and an install predating the column (migration `20260514010000`, added with no backfill) never had one at all. Before the fallback existed, `startUpgrade` threw on those rows BEFORE touching `installStatus`, so they stayed `active` and out of date and every `upgradeAllOutdated` fan-out re-skipped them with **no Event, no `installError` and nothing on the asset panel** — a host stranded this way sat on its original binary indefinitely while the fleet moved on. Only an EXPLICIT `credentialId` that does not resolve is still an error: an operator who names a credential is told it is wrong rather than quietly connected with another. **The transport follows the credential's TYPE, never the row's `installTransport`** — for a healthy row the two always agree (the install routes refuse a credential whose `type` does not match the chosen transport), and it is what makes the fallback work at all, because migration `20260609000000` backfilled `installTransport='winrm'` onto EVERY pre-existing Windows row and the managed credential is key-only, so honouring the column would hand a passwordless credential to `winrmConnectionFromCred` and die on "missing username or password" on exactly the rows that needed help. A WinRM credential on a non-Windows agent is refused. **An adopted credential is written back onto the row on SUCCESS and only on success** (with its transport, in the same update that returns `installStatus` to `active`): reaching the host is the proof, so a genuinely WinRM-only box fails to connect, keeps what it had recorded, and lands as `upgrade_failed` with a real reason — itself an upgradeable status. An operator override is never adopted; Polaris does not record a choice it did not make. A row the fan-out still cannot upgrade now writes an `agent.upgrade_skipped` Event against the asset, so the silence that hid this is gone. **Upgrade only** — install, reinstall and uninstall still require a credential on file (`hasInstallCredential` gates the Reinstall button; force-remove is the escape). → [narrative-44-48.md#rule-49](narrative-44-48.md#rule-49)

50. **A response the app did not write is a response with the app's headers missing — an unmatched route is ANSWERED, never dropped to the framework's default handler.** `src/app.ts` mounts a catch-all after the `/api/v1` router that calls `next(new AppError(404, "Not found"))`, so `errorHandler` answers it and the response keeps the headers `buildHelmetOptions()` already set. Reaching Express's built-in `finalhandler` instead **replaces** the `Content-Security-Policy` with its own `default-src 'none'`, and because neither `frame-ancestors` nor `form-action` falls back to `default-src`, every 404 in the app advertised unrestricted framing and form submission while every matched route carried the real policy (HawkScan Medium "CSP: Wildcard Directive" on `/robots.txt` and `/sitemap.xml`, 2026-09-10); its HTML body also echoed the request back ("Cannot GET /robots.txt"). The 404 message is **fixed, never built from the request** — nothing the caller sent is reflected. The asymmetry under `/api/v1` is deliberate and not a gap: `requireAuth` is mounted ahead of any route match, so an unknown API path answers **401** to an anonymous caller and the JSON 404 only once authenticated — the API does not tell an anonymous caller which endpoints exist. The edge is the second half of the same invariant: `deploy/nginx/polaris.conf` emits HSTS itself and `proxy_hide_header`s the upstream's copy (two `Strict-Transport-Security` headers is non-compliant per RFC 6797 §8.1 — a UA processes only the first, so "browsers take the strongest seen" was never true) and sets `server_tokens off`. A scan aimed straight at `npm run dev` cannot see the edge half at all, which is what `deploy/nginx/README-scan-harness.md` exists for. → [narrative-44-48.md#rule-50](narrative-44-48.md#rule-50)

51. **`DATABASE_URL` is a DRIVER URL — its `sslmode` value is TRANSLATED into the libpq vocabulary, never copied.** The parameter name is shared between node-postgres (which reads `DATABASE_URL`) and libpq (which `pg_dump` / `psql` read from `PGSSLMODE`); the value space is not. node-postgres accepts `no-verify`; libpq accepts only `disable` / `allow` / `prefer` / `require` / `verify-ca` / `verify-full` and exits 1 on anything else. `libpqSslMode(value)` in `utils/pgEnv.ts` maps `no-verify` → `require` — the same posture, since only `verify-ca` and `verify-full` validate the chain — and **refuses an unrecognized value rather than dropping it**, because omitting `PGSSLMODE` lets libpq fall back to `prefer` and silently downgrades an operator who asked for TLS to opportunistic TLS. The wizard is the producer: `setup/setupRoutes.ts → buildConnectionString` writes `sslmode=no-verify` whenever "Allow self-signed certificate" is ticked, and it is correct to keep doing so — the URL is for the driver. Copied through verbatim it meant **every backup path failed on every install created that way**: manual, scheduled and pre-update, plus restore (psql takes the same overlay), all behind `Database backup failed — see the server log`, with the real cause (`pg_dump: error: invalid sslmode value: "no-verify"`) visible only in the container log (Docker/Unraid install, 2026-09-11). The scripted RHEL / Ubuntu installs never saw it — `deploy/update-linux.sh` dumps as the local `postgres` user over a unix socket and builds no URL at all — so this is structurally a remote-database and container-deployment failure, and a green scripted install proves nothing about it. **This is rule 47's other half**: 47 says the client binary that got chosen must be able to work against the server; 51 says the connection parameters handed to it must be in that client's own vocabulary. Neither is checked by the other, and both failed for months behind the same sentence. → [narrative-44-48.md#rule-51](narrative-44-48.md#rule-51)

52. **TimescaleDB is part of the install, not a tuning option — its absence is a broken install from the first byte, not a problem that begins at a size threshold.** Every install path Polaris provisions creates it: `deploy/setup-rhel.sh` and `deploy/setup-ubuntu.sh` install `timescaledb-2-postgresql-17` + `timescaledb-tools`, run `timescaledb-tune` (which is what writes `shared_preload_libraries` — the extension cannot load without it), try-restart PostgreSQL so a re-run picks the config change up, and `CREATE EXTENSION` on the polaris database, **erroring out at each step rather than warning**: an install that comes up without the extension is one whose disk forecast and restore rehearsal are both wrong. `CREATE EXTENSION` needs superuser and the app user is deliberately not one, so the scripts are the only place it can happen and the app only ever **detects** — `detectTimescale()` in `services/timescaleService.ts` logs at **error** level when the extension is missing, because on a provisioned install that means something removed it. The two `-nodb` scripts do not own the database server, so they attempt the create and warn with the consequence (plus the managed-service caveat) rather than aborting a host they cannot fix from. `capacityService`'s `timescale_recommended` reason therefore carries **no size gate** — it fires at zero bytes, and the old 1 GB threshold now only chooses `watch` vs `warning`. **The plain-table path stays in the code and is not a supported mode**: it is the degraded state of an external or managed database that cannot offer the extension at all (RDS, Aurora and Cloud SQL have no Timescale; Timescale Cloud, Crunchy Bridge and Azure Postgres Flexible Server do), and it is the fallback when `drop_chunks` fails on a table that IS a hypertable. Corollary for every probe that reports the extension as a host fact: a null reading means **"nothing to compare"**, never "no TimescaleDB here" — `services/haService.ts` → `probeHostFacts()` can only fail that way when `rpm` itself is unavailable. → [narrative-44-48.md#rule-52](narrative-44-48.md#rule-52)
53. **A device a run could not read keeps the data it already had — so it is NAMED, and never folded in with one the run deliberately skipped.** A FortiGate that FMG direct-mode discovery cannot reach is not partially discovered: `processDevice` returns null when neither mgmt-IP producer yields an address, and the gate is dropped from the entire run. Every field discovery owns — `osVersion`, subnets, DHCP leases, the FortiSwitch/FortiAP roster — keeps its last value and keeps presenting it as current, because the projection writes are `!== null`-guarded (`if (fwProjected.osVersion !== null)`) precisely so an absent read never BLANKS a good value; the same guard means an absent read never corrects one either. A firewall's `osVersion` has exactly one writer (the discovery projection — `snmp-sysdescr` parses one vendor and it is not Fortinet), so nothing else ever heals it. Meanwhile monitoring dials `Asset.ipAddress` with a possibly per-asset REST credential — a different address and a different credential from the ones discovery uses — so the gate polls green throughout: **"it is being monitored fine" is not evidence that anything has read it.** Hence the two halves. (a) `skippedOfflineCount` and `skippedErrorCount` are different states and no surface may sum them into one "skipped" figure: offline is routine (a staged gate awaiting deployment sits offline in FMG for weeks and discovery reads its cached CMDB **on purpose**), unread means the device was never reached. `public/js/app.js` and the Discovery Activity widget render them as separate `· N offline` / `· N unread` parts. (b) `RunAccumulator.skippedErrorDevices` collects the names and `runDiscovery` writes one warning-level `integration.discover.devices_unread` Event before the abort/complete branch — in memory, because the run that collects the names is the run that writes them; a `discover.device.complete` RETRACTS an earlier error for that device, since `processDevice` logs its first direct-REST failure before deciding whether to re-resolve and retry. The incident: a prod 1801F **HA pair** showed pre-upgrade firmware while FMG was healthy and itself showed the correct version (2026-09-14). `buildFmgWarmCacheIps` keyed the mgmt-IP map on `Asset.hostname` while `processDevice` reads it back by `fmgNameKey(deviceName)` — the parent-key mismatch of prod 2026-08-12 in a new shape (a map BUILT from Asset rows, READ by FMG device name) — and the fallback `resolveDeviceMgmtIp` reads only the interface named by the integration-wide `mgmtInterface` and rejects `0.0.0.0`, the normal state of an HA cluster's dedicated management interface whose per-member addresses live under `config system ha` → `set ha-mgmt-interfaces`. Both lookups missed, every run, silently. Two fixes that look obvious and are **forbidden**: falling back to `rawDevice.ip` (FMG's device-record IP can be a public/NAT address) and falling back to the FMG proxy transport (turns "I disabled proxy" into "except when something is wrong, in which case it silently re-enables and overruns FMG's session limit"). Fix the key, or surface the skip. → [narrative-44-48.md#rule-52](narrative-44-48.md#rule-53)
54. **A region tag is stripped when no region answers to its name AND Polaris recorded retiring that name** — both halves, never just the first. `mapRegionService` keeps a companion Setting blob, `mapRegionRetiredNames`, holding `{name, regionId, retiredAt, reason: "rename"|"delete"}`; a name is appended **inside the same locked transaction that renames or deletes the region**, before any tag rotation is attempted, and `sweepRetiredRegionTags` (called by `reconcileMapRegions`, so it rides the 6-hour job, discovery Phase 13 and the map-save review alike) strips `region:<name>` from every asset and subnet still carrying it, then forgets the name. Both halves are needed because the two existing strip paths are blind to exactly this case BY DESIGN: the reconcile removes only pairs `RegionTagAssignment` recorded, and provenance is keyed by **region id** — which a rename does not change and a delete drops outright — while `stripOutOfRegionFirewallTags` judges only pinned FIREWALLS, and only against a region that still EXISTS. So a rename or delete whose tag rotation died part-way left tags **no code path could ever see again**: prod 2026-09 carried 1,492 asset tags and 114 subnet tags under two dead names ("Eastern Middle Tennessee", "Middle Tennessee") until they were removed by hand-written SQL. The obvious fix — strip every `region:` tag matching no region — is the one thing this must NOT do, because the standing contract (the `mapRegionService` entry in `polaris-change-impact`) is that manual attachments survive every reconciler forever, and an operator could hand-apply `region:Narnia` to a printer. **New ones can no longer be made** — the `Tag` registry refuses the prefix by name in every category, and asset writes refuse a newly ADDED `region:` tag naming no region (`assertAddedRegionTagsNameARegion`, a diff so that existing tags round-trip and hand-applying a LIVE region's tag stays legal) — but neither guard is retroactive and neither touches the database, so on any install with history the two sets still differ. Bounding the sweep by names Polaris itself retired keeps both promises at once: a tag naming a region that never existed is never swept. **A name that is live again is RECLAIMED, not stripped** (deleted and redrawn under the same name — the intent the delete route already assumes when it leaves principal scopes in place), matched case-insensitively. **A name whose strip throws stays on the list** and is retried next pass; dropping an unswept name puts its tag back out of reach, which is the bug this rule exists to end. The strips run outside the blob lock (thousands of rows must not hold a lock every region write needs) and only the bookkeeping rewrite takes it, re-reading the list inside the transaction so a rename racing the sweep is not discarded — rule 20a's lost-update shape applied to the second blob. **Not retroactive**: an install carrying tags stranded BEFORE this shipped has no retired-name row for them, so those need the one-off SQL in the narrative. → [narrative-44-48.md#rule-54](narrative-44-48.md#rule-54)
55. **An address places a device behind a gate only when nothing has seen it, and every surface says which answer it got** — where a device's upstream FortiGate is concerned, IPAM (`Subnet.fortigateSerial` / `fortigateDevice` → `resolveOwningGateContexts` in `services/ipUpstreamChainService.ts`, rule 41's serial-before-name precedence) is the LAST source consulted, never a replacement for evidence. **Two consumers, both last-resort:** `services/assetUpstreamService.ts` answers the General tab's Last Seen Firewall row with the owning gate only when NO `AssetFortigateSighting` names one, tagging it `source: "subnet"` with the containing `subnetCidr` and deliberately NO `lastSeen` — an inference has no moment, and a row headed "Last Seen" must never print one for a device nothing has seen; `services/dependencyTreeService.ts` adds it as the FOURTH endpoint-parent tier (`detectedVia: "subnet"`) behind `lastSeenSwitch` → `lastSeenAp` → the freshest resolving sighting. **It can only ever ADD a parent, never move one**: it is consulted solely for endpoints the three observed tiers left unplaced, and an unplaced endpoint had no parent at all, so the failure mode is a missed alert (a device held behind a gate it does not really sit behind) and never a false one — entering suppression still needs that gate CONFIRMED down under rule 38. **Two refusals:** the endpoint's claim on its address must be current under rule 40's model (`claimIsFresh` — a recycled DHCP address would otherwise parent a departed device to whoever serves that range today), and an address in no known network, or one whose owning gate has no Asset row, yields nothing rather than a guess. **The two consumers rank the same two sources oppositely, on purpose** — `ipContextService.pickNamedGate` puts the subnet ABOVE a sighting on the Add Asset panel, which answers "what is at this address today" where the serving gate is the better answer. The resolver itself is shared by all three so the serial-before-name precedence cannot drift. Permission-wise the fallback reads `Subnet` (`subnets:read`), not the sighting table (`assetsQuarantine:read`), and `visibility.subnetGate` reports which halves ran. → [narrative-44-48.md#rule-55](narrative-44-48.md#rule-55)
56. **What ignoring an alert costs is answered where the thing that costs it lives: the note belongs to the SEVERITY, the reminder belongs to the ACTION, and silence means inherit.** `NotificationRule.requireAckNote` and `NotificationRule.repeat` were both rule-level, which is correct only while every severity and every delivery of an automation deserves the same answer. Neither does. **The note** is a property of the alert record — who may close it out and on what terms — so a severity band may state its own `followUp` = `{requireAckNote}` and `effectiveAckNoteForSeverity` resolves it for the severity the alert is SITTING at, the way `effectiveActionsForSeverity` already resolves actions. **The reminder** re-sends NOTIFY actions and nothing else (`REPEATABLE_ACTION_TYPES`), so it belongs to the notify action: `repeat` rides `notifyActionEscalatableSchema` — the firing lists only, never an escalation tier (which has its own `repeatEveryMin`), never `resetActions` or `bandNotify.resolvedActions` (a recovery has nothing to chase) — and `repeatForAction` resolves it. "Page the on-call every five minutes and leave the nightly digest alone" is one automation with two honest answers, and no per-automation or per-severity cadence can say it. **Both tests are PRESENCE, never truthiness**: a band declaring `requireAckNote: false` and an action declaring `repeat: null` are ANSWERS ("not here", "this one does not chase"), while a band or action carrying no key at all is every automation authored before this and must keep inheriting, or the release would have silently switched reminders and note requirements off across the fleet. The resolution is not optional at any reader. The sweep keys each action's progress `a<i>:repeat` (`repeatStateKey`) beside that action's escalation tiers, reads the legacy bare `repeat` key once as the seed for an inheriting action so an alert live across the upgrade keeps its clock, widens rule inclusion AND the `minAfterMin` due-candidate cutoff through `allRepeatsOf` (both run before any notification is looked at, so an automation whose only reminder is on one action of one band would otherwise never be queried for), and caches its quiet-time answer per rule + severity + ACTION because the windows live inside each action's own `repeat`. `followUpPolicy` advertises the SOONEST of the repeating actions' clocks — the same rule its escalation half already applies to tiers, and for the same reason: one alert carries one snapshotted sentence while its actions may chase independently. Every acknowledge path resolves through `ackNotePolicyOf`, which is why `acknowledgeNotifications` FETCHES its candidates instead of counting them — no SQL predicate on the rule row can answer a question about a JSON band. The builder writes a `followUp` onto every band while "use different actions for each severity level" is on and strips them all when it is off (the contract band `actions` already have), and writes an explicit `repeat` onto every notify row it shows the control on, retiring `NotificationRule.repeat` for that automation as it does — a migrate-on-edit that runs only when the operator has actually looked at the step. → [narrative-44-48.md#rule-56](narrative-44-48.md#rule-56)

57. **A sub-asset alerts only if the operator pinned it — the pin IS the statement of what may alert.** Rule 37 answers this for a DEVICE (`monitored`); this is the same question one level down, for the parts of a device that carry their own dimension. Three pin arrays on `Asset` answer it — `monitoredInterfaces`, `monitoredIpsecTunnels`, `monitoredStorage` — through three exported predicates in `services/notificationEngine.ts` (`interfaceIsPinned` / `tunnelIsPinned` / `storageIsPinned`) applied by **every** resolver that reads a dimensioned sample table: the four interface state fields (`ifOperStatus` / `ifAdminStatus` / `ifIpAddress` / `poeStatus`), the four interface counter metrics, `ipsecStatus` + `ipsecThroughputBps`, and `storageUsedBytes` / `storageUsedPct` / `storageDaysUntilFull`. **The pin is a gate, never a side effect of retention.** Every one of these streams writes samples for the UNPINNED members too — `cadence:"slow"`, 24h, no rollups, but rewritten on every scrape, so they are permanently inside the engine's lookback and can never be relied on to age out. Interfaces looked gated for a while because the 2026-08 cutover made their sample table pinned-only; that was a storage decision standing in for a rule, and the window between un-pinning a port and its last rows expiring could still alert. Each dimension was closed in turn — tunnels and the interface counters in 2026-08, storage in 2026-09, the last one to go and the one that hurt most, because a device reports every filesystem it has: a fleet-wide "disk over 90%" automation alerted on removable volumes, ISO mounts, recovery partitions, mapped network drives and archive shares that are full BY DESIGN. **There is no opt-out** — bar the single value carved out below — and un-pinning is how alerting stops. The corollary the sweep needs: an absent pin is a **configuration edge the operator made, not a collection gap**, so `pinTestForTrigger` hands the pin predicate to `clearVanishedStates` and a firing row whose pin is gone clears even on a tick where the asset produced no readings at all — without it, un-pinning a device's only alerting interface stranded its alert forever. That is also what makes the Assets page's **Mass Pinning** section safe to unpin in bulk with no alert-cleanup path of its own. **The builder's pickers list the pin set, not the inventory** (`services/notificationDimensionService.ts` — nouns "monitored interfaces" / "monitored IPsec tunnels" / "monitored storage mounts"), so a filter that could never fire cannot be authored, and an empty list reads as the gate rather than as "this device has no ports". **The consequence to state before shipping a new gate**: a rule scoped to devices with an EMPTY pin set goes silent and its live alerts retire as `system:out-of-scope`. That is the intended reading of an empty pin set — nothing here was marked for monitoring — but it is a fleet-wide behaviour change every time, so it is announced, not discovered. A new dimensioned sample stream inherits all of this: a fourth pin array needs its predicate, its resolver gate, its `pinTestForTrigger` arm and its picker source, or it ships ungated. **One VALUE is carved out: `poeStatus == fault` / `== other-fault`** (2026-09-17, `poeFaultCoversUnpinned` in `services/notificationEngine.ts`) — a PoE fault condition reads every PoE-capable port, pinned or not. The gate's whole argument is that a device reports every port it has and most are idle, so `ifOperStatus == down` ungated is a page of alerts about ports nobody plugged into; that argument does not survive contact with this value. RFC 3621 gives an empty port `searching` and a switched-off one `disabled`, so the only way to read `fault` is for the PSE to have DETECTED a powered device and failed to power it — the device's own alarm bit, which business rule 24 says to prefer over a threshold we invented, and the one PoE event an operator cannot discover another way, because the AP or camera on the far end simply never comes up. Requiring every port on every switch to be pinned to catch it would defeat the gate everywhere else. Five things bind to the carve-out. (a) **`==` only.** `!= delivering` also covers fault, and covers `searching` and `disabled` with it — every empty port in the fleet, which is the storm the gate exists to stop; the wider rule is still authorable, it just stays pinned-only. (b) **The readings come from `AssetInterface`, not the sample table**, because `asset_interface_samples` HAS no unpinned rows (the 2026-06 cutover stopped writing them) — this is not a gate that could be lifted by deleting a line, the data for these ports lives in exactly one place. The two sources are UNIONED: a pinned port keeps its sample-backed reading with its real series and fast-cadence timestamp, so nothing that works today is coarsened, and only the unpinned ports are new. (c) **An unpinned reading has no series**, so a `forPolls` hold counts through the firing row's own counter anchored on `AssetInterface.lastSeen` — which counts FULL SCRAPES (default 600s), not the 60s fast pass, and the wizard's placeholder says so. A 48h backstop on `lastSeen` keeps a device that stops being scraped from holding the alert for good. (d) **`pinTestForTrigger` returns null for it**, or the vanished-state sweep would retire every unpinned-port alert on its first pass — so un-pinning stops a `== searching` rule and not a `== fault` one, and the builder's port picker switches to the PoE inventory (`sourceFor` in `notificationDimensionService.ts`, noun "PoE-capable interfaces") so the ports the rule can fire on are the ports it offers. (e) **Its RESET leaves inherit the coverage** (`coverUnpinnedPoe`, business rule 32(b)): the seeded reset is the trigger inverted, `!= fault` can never qualify under (a), and a reset tree that cannot see the faulted port either strands the alert or — through the per-asset fallback it used to fall into — lets a healthy port on the same switch clear it. Announced, not discovered, per the paragraph above: an existing `poeStatus == fault` automation started covering unpinned ports on the next tick with no edit. **The one deliberate exception is SD-WAN** (`AssetPerfSlaSample` — the three health-check metrics and `sdwanMemberState`): a health-check member is an SLA object configured ON THE GATE, a handful per firewall, not one of forty-eight ports nobody chose, so there is no pin array and none is wanted — the table carries no pinned-subset concept at all (`cadence` always `"fast"`), and what keeps it safe instead is that a member the gate stops probing produces no reading and its alert retires through the vanished-state sweep. → [narrative-44-48.md#rule-57](narrative-44-48.md#rule-57)

58. **A region tag naming no region strands the whole ranking, so level routing abstains rather than promoting the container** — `recipientDeviceRegionLevels` is ASSET-RELATIVE (level 1 = the device's own innermost region), and `deviceRegionsAtLevels` establishes "innermost" by seeding from the snapshot ∩ catalogue and filtering out the entries that are ancestors of another entry. A tag naming no current region cannot be placed in the containment forest at all — so before this rule it was simply dropped from the seed, and the ranking was computed over what remained. That is not the harmless "contributes nothing" the function's own docstring claimed: dropping the LEAF promotes its CONTAINER to level 1, and the automation pages the division instead of the site, with the site's own people hearing nothing. **It is undetectable from every surface an operator has**: the stale tag still renders on the asset page, the automation stays enabled, the email still sends, `Notification.regionTags` still records the tag, and only the TIER is wrong. Prod ran exactly this on 2026-09-14 — 3,587 FortiSwitches carried `region:Middle Tenneessee` (a name retired under rule 54, four e's), so every down alert for them resolved L1 to `Southern Division` and mailed the division pair while the two people scoped to the site were never reached; the automation had been "working" by every check the UI could offer, and it took reading `notification_deliveries.target` against the map-region catalogue to see it. So `deviceRegionsAtLevels` now returns `[]` **for every requested level** when `orphanedRegionTags` finds any unplaceable tag, and the caller (`notificationRecipientService.usersForTarget`) logs which tag stranded it. **Abstention is scoped to the level arm alone** — `recipientUserIds`, `recipientRoles`, `recipientRegions`, `recipientDeviceRegion`, `recipientTags` and address-book contacts on the same action all still resolve, so the alert is never lost, only its level-scoped tier withheld until the tag is rotated or the region redrawn. The alternative — treating the orphan as an opaque leaf — was rejected because an unplaceable name has no containment edge, so it would silently redefine what every HIGHER level on that asset means. **Three distinct silences, three distinct log lines** (orphaned tag / no region at the requested depth / regions matched but nobody carries them): they read identically from the outside and are fixed differently, and the whole failure class here is a routing arm that reaches nobody without saying so. The same pass gave the email branch the warning its `web_push` sibling already had, for the case that hides behind a correct-looking Users page: a recipient resolved by any arm whose account has **no email address** is dropped by `buildAddressOwnerMap` and the builder only ever warned about missing PUSH devices. Rule 54 is the other half of this — it stops new tags being stranded; this one stops a stranded tag from quietly repointing an escalation. → [narrative-44-48.md#rule-58](narrative-44-48.md#rule-58)

59. **The controller's view of its own link is a second opinion, and an unreadable controller has no view at all** — what the parent FortiGate says about its FortiLink session to a managed FortiSwitch (or its CAPWAP tunnel to a managed FortiAP) is projected onto four `Asset` columns — `fortilinkStatus` (`up` | `down` | `unknown`) / `fortilinkStatusRaw` / `fortilinkCheckedAt` / `fortilinkChangedAt` — by `jobs/sweepFortinetLinkState.ts` → `services/fortinetLinkStateService.ts`. **Columns, not a key on `fortinetTopology`**: discovery rewrites that blob wholesale every cycle and a 60s sweep read-modify-writing it would lose-update the stamp (the region-blob trap), and scalars let `resolveAssetStateReadings` read the field off the scope row like `monitorStatus`. It sweeps **every** FortiGate-managed switch/AP regardless of polling method, which is the point: `probeFortinetController` already renders this verdict but only for `responseTimePolling === "rest_api"` assets, where it IS their `monitorStatus` — the devices it tells you something new about are the ICMP/SNMP-polled ones, where **a dead FortiLink session still answers every ping**. Cost is bounded by CONTROLLER count via monitoringService's shared 30s inventory cache (≤2 calls per controller per tick at any fleet size; ~1.5/min per kind where the probe path already reads the same controller, since the two 60s timers coalesce only when they land in one window); `POLARIS_FORTILINK_SWEEP_SEC` raises it. **Three refusals**: an unreadable controller writes NOTHING (not `down`, not even a `checkedAt` refresh — an expired token must not report a fleet-wide link outage; failures are isolated per controller); answered-but-absent is **`unknown`, never `down`** (a post-config-push window looks identical, and down-detection authority is rule 36's); and it **never touches `monitorStatus`/`consecutiveFailures`** — the column's value is that it may DISAGREE with the monitor pill. As an `asset_state` automation field it takes two departures from the Asset-column fields beside it: a **null produces no reading at all** (not a reading of null, which would make `!= up` true of every workstation in a fleet-wide scope, and which lets the vanished-state sweep clear alerts on a device that stops being managed), and the reading anchor is **`fortilinkCheckedAt`, not `lastMonitorAt`** (a `forPolls` hold must count times the CONTROLLER answered). Confirmations refresh `checkedAt` and leave `changedAt` alone, so "down for 2h 13m" is the outage and not the poll age. Transitions write `asset.fortilink.changed` unconditionally (the `asset.*.changed` family), `warning` on the way down and `info` on the way up and on first observation. Rule 37 still gates alerting: a managed switch with `monitored=false` shows the row and never fires. → [narrative-44-48.md#rule-59](narrative-44-48.md#rule-59)
60. **A footer that tells the reader who else knows must never name a Bcc — and the only reason it cannot is that `target` is the To line.** The alert email footer answers "who else has heard about this", because an alert reaching two transports reaches two audiences that cannot see each other and the answer decides whether the reader picks the device up. `{push.recipients}` named the web-push half; `{email.recipients}` names the mail half, and it has to exist because **no single copy's To header is the whole audience of the send** — a second notify action in the same fire mails its own list, the push half names people the email never reached at all, and a Cc rider is a reader the To line does not name, so a reader who checks the To line to see who else got this message gets a confidently partial answer. (Two more reasons are gone: a send used to split per recipient timezone and per acknowledge capability, and on 2026-09-15 both were removed — for this same reason read the other way round, an operator seeing one address on their own To line beside a footer naming two. The line survived because the three above never depended on a split.) **Both lines scope to the SEND, not to the alert** (changed 2026-09-16; they were alert-wide when the rule was written). A send is one `executeActions` fan-out, identified by the `dispatch` stamp `expandDeliveries` writes into every row's `meta`, and `buildRecipientBlocks(notificationId, dispatchId)` narrows on it **in the query** — a weekend outage reminding every five minutes leaves hundreds of rows on one alert. The fan-out is the grain rather than the delivery row, which is what keeps the feature alive: a fire that mails the NOC and pushes the on-call does both in one call, so the NOC's copy still says the phone buzzed. Alert scope was the original decision and it was wrong in a way only the reader could see — an operator read a reminder whose footer said "Email sent to \<the escalation manager\>" and concluded their reminders were going over their head, when the manager sat on the T+30 tier and on no reminder at all. A footnote at the bottom of one email is read as a claim about that email, whatever the header comment says. A row carrying no stamp — queued before this shipped, still draining — falls back to the alert-wide read rather than losing its footer, and `tests/unit/alertFooterSendScope.test.ts` pins the narrowing, the one-call-one-dispatch grain and that fallback. Both lines count ROWS rather than outcomes, and are DEFERRED — the delivery rows they read are created by `expandDeliveries` AFTER the body was composed, the email half reading rows the very drain that fills it is in the middle of sending. **The Bcc rule is the load-bearing one.** A blind copy that appears in a footer every recipient reads has stopped being blind, and the operator who Bcc'd someone has been silently overruled by a footnote. The extraction is safe **only** because both email paths put To — and only To — in `NotificationDelivery.target`: the composed path joins the whole To line into one row, the plain path writes one address, and `toAddressesOf` therefore cannot reach a Bcc no matter what it parses. Cc IS named, being visible to everyone on that copy already, and it comes from `meta.cc` alone — `meta.bcc` is read by nothing. **So the invariant is not "the renderer filters Bcc out" but "the renderer is never handed one", and that is a property of `expandDeliveries`, not of this service**: the day a delivery row folds Bcc into `target` or into `meta.cc`, this footer unblinds it, with no error and no test failing anywhere near the change. `tests/unit/alertEmailRecipients.test.ts` pins both halves for that reason. The email half deduplicates by ADDRESS rather than by account (a typed address and an address-book contact have no account at all — the same "unknown means deliver" posture rules 25 and 39 take, read the other way round) and prints the owning account's name where one holds the address; the account lookup asks for each address as written AND lower-cased, since `User.email` carries no citext. Deferral registration differs per token and the asymmetry is deliberate: `isDeferredToken` matches the `push.` PREFIX, while `email.recipients` is an ENUMERATED name in `DEFERRED_TOKEN_NAMES`, because an `email.` prefix would also swallow a future `{email.*}` token that is not deferred — and a token that is not registered is blanked at compose time, before the pass that fills it, which is the `{chart.trigger}` regression. → [narrative-60-64.md#rule-60](narrative-60-64.md#rule-60)

61. **Changing a credential ends every OTHER session on it, and rotating your own must carry the CSRF token across** — `PUT /auth/password` (`api/routes/auth.ts`) is the self-service password change, gated on nothing beyond a session because **the body names no user**: it changes the caller's own account, and proves the caller knows the current password first. That proof is the whole difference from the admin-side `PUT /users/:id/password`, which cannot make it — hence two distinct Events, the self one `user.password_changed` and the admin one `user.password_reset` at `warning`. **The point of changing a password is the revocation**: a new one is worthless while whoever learned the old one still holds a live cookie, so `revokeOtherSessions` deletes every other non-expired `session` row carrying that `userId`. It is **best-effort raw SQL against connect-pg-simple's table** — the same posture `getOnlineUserIds` in `users.ts` takes, because that table belongs to the session middleware rather than to Prisma's schema, so a read or write against it must never fail a password change that has already been committed. **The caller's own session is rotated but KEPT, and the carry is the load-bearing half.** `req.session.regenerate()` discards `csrfToken` along with everything else — but `csrfMiddleware` already mirrored the OLD token into a response cookie EARLIER in the same request, and the regenerated session only mints a replacement on the NEXT one, so a naive rotate leaves the still-open page holding a cookie nothing will match and **403s the user's next write until they reload**. `rotateSessionKeepingIdentity` therefore copies the identity fields AND `csrfToken` across. The failure is silent in every direction: the change returns 200, the page looks fine, and the breakage surfaces later on an unrelated save — nothing throws, and no test near the change fails. `tests/integration/selfChangePassword.test.ts` pins it by making a SECOND change over the same agent and the same token after the first rotated the session underneath it; `authedAgent` in `tests/integration/_helpers.ts` documents the same trap from the login side, where a full page navigation hides it. **Rate-limited at the login ceiling** (`passwordChangeLimiter`, 10 / 15 min): a body carrying the caller's current password is the login surface again, just behind a cookie, and it is reachable without ever touching the login limiter. Local accounts only — every other `authProvider` is refused with the reason, the directory owning that credential. → [narrative-60-64.md#rule-61](narrative-60-64.md#rule-61)

62. **An install is identified by something it persists, never by the name the runtime handed the process** — the active-instance heartbeat (`services/haHeartbeatService.ts`) is the last of the four layers that stop two Polaris instances sharing one database (docs/HA.md §5), and it spent its first release identifying an instance by `os.hostname()`. In a container that name is the **container id**, which the runtime regenerates on every recreate — so every image upgrade read the stamp the previous container had written seconds earlier, found a name that was not its own, and refused to boot: `Refusing to start: another host holds a fresh active-instance heartbeat`. One install, one database, no second instance anywhere. It self-healed after the 90s window only where the restart policy retried; on Unraid, plain `docker run` or a foreground compose it stayed down until an operator intervened. So identity is now `resolveInstanceId()` — `POLARIS_HA_INSTANCE_ID`, else a uuid persisted at `<STATE_DIR>/data/instance-id`, else (only when that file cannot be written) the hostname, with a warning naming the consequence. The state directory is the bind mount or install root that outlives the process, which is exactly the lifetime an install has; two hosts pointed at one database still hold two state directories and so two ids, so the case the guard exists for is untouched. Three consequences bind together. (a) **A stamp carrying no `instanceId` still compares on hostname** — it was written by a release predating the field, that peer may genuinely be live, and treating an unknown stamp as "probably me" would open the precise hole the guard closes; the cost is one 90s wait on the upgrade that introduces the id, and nothing after. (b) **A clean shutdown releases the claim**: `releaseActiveInstance()` on SIGTERM/SIGINT deletes the row only when the stored stamp is ours — never a peer's — and latches so an in-flight tick cannot resurrect it, which is why a restart, an upgrade or a promotion waits for nothing, while a `kill -9` leaves the stamp and the window applies as designed. (c) **Anything that identifies a NODE must be excluded from the HA standby sync**: `deploy/ha/ha-rsync-exclude` copies everything it does not list, so without its `data/instance-id` line the standby would inherit the primary's identity and this layer would silently stop distinguishing the two nodes — undetectable in operation, because a guard that never fires looks identical to a guard that cannot. → [narrative-60-64.md#rule-62](narrative-60-64.md#rule-62)

63. **The complexity bar belongs to the operator, and a password that no longer meets it is replaced on the FAR side of the second factor** — the five rules Polaris shipped with (min 8 + four character classes) became `passwordPolicyConfig`, a Setting row edited at Users → Authentication → Settings. Two consequences follow. **First, the Zod schema stopped being the bar**: `passwordPolicySchema` (`utils/password.ts`) is now a SHAPE check — non-empty, ≤1024 chars as a DoS guard — because a Zod object is built at import time and cannot read a Setting row, and each of the four password-writing routes (admin create, admin reset, the self-service change, the setup wizard's first admin) follows its `.parse()` with `assertPasswordMeetsPolicy()`. A complexity rule reappearing in that schema means two places enforce the bar and only one of them is configurable. The service **fails to the DEFAULTS, never to "no policy"** — the opposite of `loginAccessService`'s fail-open, because there failing safe means letting people IN while here it means keeping the bar UP — and `minLength` clamps to **[8, 128]** (NIST SP 800-63B's floor) at parse and at save, so a UI that offered 1 is not reachable. Turning every character class off is a legitimate posture, not a misconfiguration. **Second, `forceChangeOnLogin` is answered against the PLAINTEXT, and only after every factor.** A stored hash is one-way, so the single moment Polaris can judge an existing password is the login where its owner types it; the alternative (a "changed before the policy" timestamp) would interrupt users who already comply. But the demand must never be made in FRONT of the second factor: a password-change token minted at the password step would let someone holding a stolen password set a new one — and collect the withheld session — without ever facing TOTP or a passkey, which is an MFA bypass wearing a policy's clothes. So the flag rides from the password step to the far side of the second factor on the pending token (`utils/mfaPending.ts`, `purpose: "mfa"` carrying `mustChangePassword`), and only there is a `purpose: "password-change"` token issued. That token buys exactly one thing — set a conforming password, receive the session — is single-use, is consumed only after the write lands (a failed update leaves the user able to retry rather than stranded at a login screen), and is refused at the TOTP endpoint and vice versa. `tests/integration/forcedPasswordChange.test.ts` pins the ordering with a TOTP-enrolled account; it is the regression test for the bypass, not a nicety. → [narrative-60-64.md#rule-63](narrative-60-64.md#rule-63)

64. **A passkey is bound to the origin that issued its challenge, the install decides what a passkey is FOR, and it never names an account that does not already exist** — WebAuthn credentials (`UserPasskey`, `services/passkeyService.ts`) are local-accounts-only, enforced at the route and re-checked at verification: an SSO account's credentials belong to its identity provider, and a Polaris-owned second way in would be invisible to whoever thinks they control it centrally. **The install decides the role** via `passkeyConfig.mode` — `off` / `login` (passwordless) / `second-factor` / `both` (the default, because enabling passkeys REFUSES nothing: passwords keep working and registration is opt-in per user). Under a mode that includes `second-factor`, registering a passkey makes that account's login two-step exactly as enrolling TOTP does, so `DELETE /users/:id/passkeys` exists for the same reason the TOTP reset does. **`requireUserVerification` (default true) is what licenses a passkey to be the whole login** — UV is two factors in one gesture — and the passwordless route mirrors the flag into `session.mfaVerified` rather than asserting verification that did not happen. **The RP is derived from the request, not configured**: TLS terminates anywhere, `POLARIS_PUBLIC_URL` may be unset and one install is legitimately reached by several names, so `utils/webauthnRp.ts` reads the Host header — safe only because the browser refuses any ceremony whose rpId is not a registrable suffix of the page's own origin, so a forged Host can make a ceremony fail and nothing else. The rpId and origin are then **pinned into the ceremony at issue time** (`utils/webauthnChallenge.ts`) and replayed at verify; re-deriving would check the assertion against whatever the SECOND request claimed to be. Two deployment shapes cannot host passkeys at all — plain HTTP off localhost, and an IP-address install — and both return a REASON rather than throwing, so the UI says which instead of surfacing a browser `SecurityError`. Two MORE shapes are misconfigured proxies wearing those refusals: TLS terminated at a proxy with `TRUST_PROXY` unset (`req.protocol` reads http, so `forwardedProtoClaim` looks for the proxy's own `X-Forwarded-Proto` / `X-Forwarded-Scheme` / `Forwarded` / `X-Forwarded-Ssl` claim and names TRUST_PROXY in the reason — never honoring it, since a spoofable header must not grant what `req.secure` withheld), and a proxy that REWRITES Host (the derived rpId then covers no browser's origin, which only the client can see: `PolarisWebAuthn.unavailableHere(rpId)` in `public/js/webauthn.js` compares it to `location.hostname`, the account modal prints the reason and the login page just withholds the button). **The passwordless options endpoint names no credentials**: `allowCredentials` is empty so it cannot answer "does alice exist, and how many keys has she", which is why registration demands a resident key; the second-factor step DOES name them, the caller having already proved the password, and binds the assertion to that account (`expectUserId`) so no passkey-holder can finish someone else's half-done login. Every refusal — unknown credential, bad signature, SSO-managed account, a signature counter that went BACKWARDS from a non-zero value (WebAuthn's one clone signal; 0 → 0 is normal and means nothing) — returns the SAME message. And unlike every other credential path in Polaris, **it never provisions**: a discoverable credential resolving to no row is an authentication failure, not a signup. A pre-session ceremony endpoint needs four registrations to be correct — the route, `LOGIN_CREDENTIAL_PATHS` in `app.ts` if it can end in a session, a CSRF exemption whose segment boundary does not swallow `/passkeys/register` and `/passkeys/:id`, and a `passkeyCeremonyLimiter` mount — and each one missing fails in a different silent direction. → [narrative-60-64.md#rule-64](narrative-60-64.md#rule-64)

65. **A delivery test is a SPECIMEN of the alert, not a rehearsal against live inventory — every fact in it is invented, and it says so where no template can delete it** — the automation wizard's "Send Test Email" used to fire the draft against whichever real asset it would match (`previewRule` picked the device that would actually fire, on the reasoning that a test should look exactly like the real thing), so the message carried a live hostname, management IP, site code and the device's own admin description. That email is sent ON DEMAND, by anyone holding `automationManagement:write`, to an address they type at the keyboard, and it lands in an inbox nobody treats as inventory — which makes "looks exactly like the real thing" a property worth far less than the one it costs. So `utils/sampleAlertDevice.ts` supplies the whole device (`SAMPLE_ALERT_DEVICE` + `sampleDimensionFor(metric)` for the sensor / SD-WAN member / port a test should name), drawn from the ranges reserved for documentation so nothing in a test email can collide with a real one: IPv4 from 192.0.2.0/24 (RFC 5737 TEST-NET-1), MAC from the RFC 7042 00:00:5E:00:53:xx block, every name "Example"-prefixed. Four consequences bind to it. (a) **`Notification.assetId` is null** — a test belongs to no device, so it can never surface on a real asset's alert list, and `{asset.link}` renders empty so `pruneDeadLinks` drops the "Open device" button rather than linking at a device page that does not exist. (b) **No reading is quoted**: `triggerSummary` is called with `value: null` and falls back to stating the CONDITION ("Response time (median over 5 minutes) is above 500 ms"), which is the wording the operator is testing anyway — the only honest number available would be some real device's, and a made-up one would read as a measurement. (c) **The charts are generated, not queried** (`alertChartService.sampleChartSeries`, behind `buildAlertCharts(null, …, {sampleData: true})`): pure, deterministic, shaped like each real counterpart, and inventing NO fail spans or SD-WAN down spans, because those bands mean "nothing was measured here" / "the device declared this dead" and teaching that about a device which does not exist is the wrong lesson. (d) **The TEST marking is applied at DELIVERY, never as a template token.** `markEmailAsTest` (`utils/alertEmailTemplate.ts`) stamps the subject, an HTML banner above the card and a block above the plain-text body inside `notificationDeliveryService.emailMessageFor`, on both compose branches, after every substitution and pruning pass — so it survives a fully customized email body, and a body customized before the marking existed carries it too. A `{test.notice}` token was considered and rejected for exactly that reason: on the one email that must never be mistaken for an outage, the marking cannot be something the operator's own template can delete. The `[TEST]` prefix in `Notification.message` stays what marks the push and chat bodies, which print the message directly. A new `{asset.*}` token needs its field added to `SAMPLE_ALERT_DEVICE` as well as to the engine's `ASSET_DETAIL_SELECT`, or the test email prunes a row the real alert prints — which is how a specimen quietly stops being faithful. → [narrative-60-64.md#rule-65](narrative-60-64.md#rule-65)
66. **A measurement window may be counted in READINGS, and then the hold counts recalculations of it** — `windowSec` takes whatever samples landed in a span of time, so an aggregated automation's SAMPLE SIZE is a function of how well the device happens to be working: `avg` over an hour is sixty readings on a healthy device and seventeen on one dropping three quarters of its probes, under the same threshold and the same name. **`windowPolls` states the window as the last N samples that produced a value instead** (`rollingAggregate`, `triggerWindowPolls`), so what stretches under loss is the wall clock the window spans rather than the number of measurements in it — its defining test is the equality that a holed series and a clean series carrying the same N values produce the same number. A miss is not counted, not filled and not fabricated: filling with **0 ms** flatters a dying device (the FortiAP that prompted this read 507 ms over the polls it answered and 144 ms with its misses as zeros, so the alert would have CLEARED as the device got worse; median does not rescue that — past 50% loss the zeros ARE the distribution and the median is 0), and filling with the **probe timeout** poisons a latency metric into a loss alarm. A failed probe's latency is UNDEFINED, which is what every other NMS keeps (Nagios `check_icmp`'s `rta`/`pl` pair, SmokePing's median-of-replies with loss as the line colour, the SRE convention of measuring latency over successful requests only); loss is business rule 29's metric and down is business rule 36's. **The hold composes only here.** Under a time window `forPolls` is refused — the window IS the period, and a second clock would be two clocks doing one job — while a count window cuts the readings into DISJOINT groups of N, so `reduceReadings` hands the group aggregates on AS the series and `forPolls` means M consecutive GROUPS over the line. **The groups step by N, never by 1**, and that is the load-bearing choice: consecutive ROLLING windows would overlap by N-1 samples, so three of them agreeing is three near-identical numbers and barely more evidence than one, while three disjoint groups are three INDEPENDENT looks at the device. It also makes the wall clock legible — time to alert is groupSize x sustained polls, which is what the builder labels promise ("Poll Group Size" x "Sustained for (poll groups)") — and it is why `lookbackMsFor` sizes a count window from the PRODUCT of the two counts rather than their sum. A trailing partial group is dropped for the same reason a partial window is. Every existing mechanism then works untouched: `leadingRun` counts the run and `tierRuns` gives each severity tier its own against the same smoothed series (business rule 19). That is what a time window cannot express — one 1500 ms spike inside an otherwise healthy 5-reading window never clears the threshold, so the counter never starts. **Too few readings is NO reading**, never a partial window (a different statistic under the same threshold), which is also what bounds the cost: `lookbackMsFor` reaches over the wall-clock mirror of both counts DOUBLED — enough for 50% loss — and a device worse than that abstains, a fleet-scale decision (this fetch is the heaviest thing an automation does at 2000 assets) rather than a correctness one. Both counts keep their mirrors (`windowSec`, `forDurationSec`): they size the fetch and feed the prose, the builder always writes them, and an API-authored rule omitting them gets the full 6-hour lookback rather than a guessed cadence. The builder states the unit the rule stores — "Measured over" with a minutes/polls picker, the breach counter shown only beside a count window or a ratio's History (the two windows with a free hold axis) — and `tgStampWindowPolls` STRIPS the count from every leaf that must not carry it, or a leftover would keep measuring in readings while the field, the sentence and the formula all said minutes. → [narrative-60-64.md#rule-66](narrative-60-64.md#rule-66)
67. **A missed response-time poll is the TIMEOUT it cost, and an outage RESETS the window** — response time is the one metric whose failure has a duration: every other metric's miss is an absence, while a failed probe waited the asset's full `probeTimeoutMs` and heard nothing, which is a fact about the device measured in the metric's own unit. Business rule 66 drops misses, and for a general metric that is right; for response time it lets a device answering one poll in ten read exactly as fast as one answering every poll. So a miss that did not put the asset Down is filled with **`AssetMonitorSample.timeoutMs`** — the timeout resolved for that asset AT PROBE TIME, recorded on the row by `recordProbeResult` (via `probeAsset`'s out-param and the ICMP sweep's `item.timeoutMs`) rather than re-resolved at evaluation: a window of PAST probes must use the timeout that applied to each one, and resolving monitor settings inside the engine's tick would mean widening its tight asset select at 2000 assets. A failure with no recorded timeout predates the column and stays EXCLUDED — the pre-feature behaviour, self-healing within one window. **The fill is safe only because of the reset**: through a real outage it would turn a latency metric into a loss alarm about what the down automation already owns (business rule 29h's failure, in the metric next door). `prepareResponseTimeWindow` therefore walks newest-first and DISCARDS everything at and before the most recent `assetDown` probe. `assetDown` is stamped from the status the probe RESULTS in, so the degraded/out line is the operator's own `missedPolls` (business rule 36): an amber miss still counts and is still filled, and only the misses their automation calls Down reset anything. **A recovered device therefore has NO reading until its window refills** — `rollingAggregate` refuses a partial window, so ~10 minutes of quiet at a 60s cadence: a settling period, not a blind spot, since `down` was another automation's subject throughout. Response time DEFAULTS to a count window of 10 in the builder and the unit is chosen for the operator (picking minutes for response time is picking the denominator that floats); it is a default, not a lock, asserting itself only on a draft stating no window and never after the picker is touched or on a stored rule. Existing rules were migrated by the **V7** one-shot (`seedBaselineAutomationsV7ResponseTimeWindowAt`), which — unlike V5 — does NOT spare edited rules, because V5 added a knob whose default was arguable while this fixes what the knob MEASURES; it names every rule and its old window in a warning Event. → [narrative-60-64.md#rule-67](narrative-60-64.md#rule-67)

68. **What Polaris ships and what the operator owns are two different kinds of MIB, and a manufacturer profile is only ever an OVERRIDE of what the generic ones already do** — the generic IETF/IEEE modules are BAKED IN (`services/stdMibs/`, read off disk by `oidRegistry.loadStandardLayer`): every install has them, nobody can delete or edit them, and they change only with a product update, which is right for standards every device speaks. A manufacturer's own MIB is SEEDED instead (`services/vendorMibs/` → `jobs/seedVendorMibs.ts`, inserted through `mibService.createMib` so it takes the identical path to an upload): it appears in the MIB Database like anything the operator uploaded and **they can delete it**, because it is the vendor's file and they may hold a newer one, object to it shipping, or not run that gear. A vendor module placed in `stdMibs/` would silently become undeletable — that directory is globbed — which is the failure `tests/unit/seedVendorMibs.test.ts` exists to prevent. **Seeding is fresh-installs-only**: an upgrade skips it (the `seedManufacturerProfilesSeededAt` marker proves a previous release ran here), because an existing MIB Database is curated by its operator and adding vendor modules for devices they may not own is editing their data. **A profile earns its place only by overriding something the generic MIBs cannot say**, which is why Cisco ships one (per-pool memory, the 5-second CPU) and MikroTik ships neither profile nor MIB (RouterOS answers CPU, memory and storage through HOST-RESOURCES-MIB). Deleting either shipped piece is supported and never silent: without the profile `pickDbProfile` returns null and the collectors fall back to HOST-RESOURCES-MIB; without the MIB the profile's rows read `unresolved` and name the module to re-upload. → [narrative-60-64.md#rule-68](narrative-60-64.md#rule-68)

69. **A reservation count is of addresses HELD, and a release is history that must never inflate it** — Polaris soft-releases: `reservationService` sets `status` to `released`, `reservationStaleService` to `expired`, and nothing but `cleanupStaleDnsResolvedReleased` (one narrow `dns_resolved` case) ever deletes a row. A reservation table is therefore an append-mostly ledger, and any count taken without a status filter answers "how many reservations has this network ever had", which on a DHCP-discovered `/24` that has cycled its leases is a number in the hundreds against 254 usable addresses. So **one numerator, stated once: `status: "active"` AND `ipAddress` not null** — reservations holding an individual address right now. `ipService.subnetCapacity`, the IP panel's footer bar and `subnetService.listSubnets`' `_count.reservations` all take it, and a fourth surface counting addresses takes it too; a whole-subnet reservation (`ipAddress` null) is excluded from all of them because it consumes no individual address and already shows as subnet status `reserved`. The **denominator** is `usableHostCount(cidr)` from `utils/cidr.ts` — never open-coded, and `null` rather than 0 for IPv6 and for any CIDR the parser refuses, because a percentage of nothing drawn as an empty bar is a positive claim that the network is free (the UI shows an em dash; `polaris-ui-canon` → canon-tables-lists.md § Utilization bar). **The unfiltered count is not wrong, it answers a different question**, and exactly one kind of caller needs it: what a cascade removes. `Subnet` deletion cascades to every reservation row and `subnetArchiveService.archiveSubnet` copies every row with no status filter, so both confirmations quote `listSubnets`' `totalReservations` — which is also what made the archive confirmation agree with the success toast it had been contradicting. → [narrative-60-64.md#rule-69](narrative-60-64.md#rule-69)

70. **Absence from a directory decommissions what that directory MANAGES, and only when the read was whole** — an operator who deletes a computer object or disables a device account is performing the decommission, so AD and Entra get vCenter's disappearance sweep: a device gone from the directory, or reported disabled by it, flips its asset to `decommissioned`. Five things produce a missing device and only one is a deletion, so the pass is **opt-in** (`decommissionMissing`, default false on both integrations — Polaris is installed by strangers and the first run of a sweep nobody asked for is a fleet-wide status change; off, it does nothing at all, stale source rows included, because deleting them is the same judgement wearing a smaller hat) and guarded three times. (a) **`adSweepBlockedReason` / `entraSweepBlockedReason`** refuse a scoped ("Discover Now"), cancelled, capped or EMPTY read — zero objects under a `baseDn` that held a fleet is a bind/consent answer far more often than an emptied domain (business rule 35's shrunken-read reasoning, `vcenterSweepBlockedReason`'s identical call), and a refusal skips the source deletes too or the next good run would have nothing left to notice. (b) **`classifyDirectoryRows`** is fed the RAW identifier set — snapshotted before `ouInclude`/`ouExclude`/`deviceInclude`/`deviceExclude` and before the `includeDisabled=false` skip, the same trick `partitionStaleVcenterSources` plays with `presentVmMorefs` — so a filter edit reads as configuration and re-widening it re-matches the same asset instead of orphaning its identity; it sorts three ways, `gone`/`disabled`/`alive`, because a DISABLED device has not left the directory and KEEPS its source row while its asset is still decommissioned. (c) **`absenceExceedsGuard`** refuses a delete set over `max(50, 20% of what this integration owns)` — `directorySyncService.deleteExceedsGuard`'s formula on assets instead of contacts — because it is the only guard that can see the failure the other two structurally cannot: a narrowed `baseDn`, an OU delegated away, a grant covering half a tenant, each returning a complete, non-empty, well-formed read missing most of the estate. **Ownership decides, not provenance.** Unlike vCenter's "any other source still claims it" test — which on a FortiGate-managed network is almost always yes, since nearly every workstation carries a `fortigate-endpoint` sighting — the judgement is `Asset.discoveredByIntegrationId`, the System tab's "Managed by". Managed by THIS integration and the directory's word is final; managed by ANOTHER and the sweep only drops its own stale row; managed by NOTHING counts as this directory's, which is load-bearing rather than an edge case because `syncEntraDevices` has never stamped ownership at all and every Entra asset is therefore unowned. An asset is judged only once EVERY row this integration holds on it agrees (an Entra device still in Intune has not left the tenant), and a kind whose endpoint was not read this cycle (Intune off, or its call failed) leaves its rows alive — not read is not not there. One shared `sweepDirectoryAbsence` in `discovery/discoveryEngine.ts` runs both, last pass, `releaseAssetsForDecommission` first so rule 16's maintenance reconcile cannot re-flip the status. Time-based aging stays with `decommissionStaleAssets`. → [narrative-60-64.md#rule-70](narrative-60-64.md#rule-70)

71. **A figure Polaris reports about itself accounts for itself, and a measurement whose mechanism broke says so instead of reading zero** — the Maintenance tab's Database card printed a total summed over EVERY relation in the database above a table list that was public-schema-only, heap-and-index-only, and — once the TimescaleDB chunk fold silently failed — hypertable-parent-only: 76.6 GB over rows summing to ~2.6 GB, with no way for an operator to tell which of the three gaps held the missing 74 GB (prod, 2026-09-17). So **one module measures, and the parts are the whole by construction**: `services/dbSizeService.ts` attributes every relation to exactly ONE bucket — an index to the table it indexes, a TOAST relation to the table it stores for, a TimescaleDB chunk (compressed or not) to its user hypertable — and the reported total is the SUM of those buckets rather than a second query, so the figure and the explanation under it cannot drift apart. Whatever is not one of Polaris's tables is still on the same disk and is therefore still listed, by name: the pg-boss queue schema, PostgreSQL's own catalog, other schemas, and an `Unattributed` residual that is zero on a healthy install. The second half is the one that cost the incident: **a degraded measurement degrades VISIBLY.** `getTableSizes` returns `sizing: "parent-only"` when the chunk fold fails, because a 15 GB sample table and an empty one both render as "0 B" once the fold is gone — a warn log nobody reads is not a report; and every breakdown carries `neverAnalyzedRelations`, because these figures are catalog reads (`pg_class.relpages`, never `pg_database_size()` — that one stat()s every chunk relfilenode and took minutes at prod scale) and a `pg_upgrade` carries no planner statistics before PG18, so an un-analyzed relation reports zero pages regardless of what it holds. This is the same principle `capacityService`'s volume scan already applies — a filesystem it cannot measure is reported degraded rather than dropped from the list, after an unmeasured `/var` filled to 100% while the card said "All capacity checks passed" — stated once and generalized to the numbers themselves. → [narrative-60-64.md#rule-71](narrative-60-64.md#rule-71)

72. **A detection script asserts every prerequisite its remediation establishes, and a mode that establishes nothing refuses instead of reporting success** — the Windows SSH onboarding pair (`services/sshOnboardingScript.ts`) checked the OpenSSH Server capability, the `sshd` service, `administrators_authorized_keys` and the key inside it, and stopped there; its Linux counterpart had always ALSO checked the account and the sudoers drop-in. Meanwhile `accountMode: "existing"` — which is the DEFAULT, because an existing account has no name to prefill — printed `Using existing account`, appended the key, and exited 0 whether or not that account was on the host. An Intune fleet therefore reported `ok: Polaris SSH onboarding present` on endpoints where the account had never existed and no logon could ever succeed (found in the field 2026-09-18, against a fleet whose remediation rows read `Not run` precisely because detection was satisfied). So **both halves name the account now**: detection verifies it exists, is enabled and is a member of local Administrators, and existing mode REFUSES — `error: account ... does not exist on this host` — rather than authorizing a key for nobody, which is exactly what `LINUX_ACCOUNT_EXISTING_SH` had always done. The membership predicate is emitted into BOTH scripts from the one `POLARIS_PS_HELPERS` constant for the same reason the key predicate is: a pair that decides "is an administrator" two different ways either oscillates forever or certifies an endpoint it cannot use. It resolves the group by SID (`S-1-5-32-544`) because the name is localized; it falls back to the WinNT provider because on an Entra-joined endpoint `Get-LocalGroupMember` either returns unresolvable members as raw `S-1-12-1-…` strings or throws outright and yields nothing; and it compares on the LEAF name because the same member reads `DOMAIN\user` from one path and bare `user` from the other. What deliberately stays OUT is the firewall: with no `polarisServerIp` configured there is no Polaris rule to find, and Windows' own rule is left in two different settled states by that same setting (business rule 76) which the detection builder is not told apart — which makes the firewall the one condition remediation could never satisfy — the boundary that stops "assert every prerequisite" becoming "assert everything" and looping the pair forever, the same reasoning that keeps an unsupported Windows build at exit 0. → [narrative-60-64.md#rule-72](narrative-60-64.md#rule-72)

73. **Planned downtime is reported as planned, and a scoped view of a window still reports the WHOLE window** — `NOT_IN_MAINTENANCE` keeps in-window assets out of every down/warning/stale feed (their `monitorStatus` is frozen, possibly at `down`, and a frozen value is not live state — business rule 16), so a dashboard built from those widgets says nothing at all about the devices that are down on purpose. `maintenanceScheduleService.getActiveMaintenanceSchedules` (the `maintenanceSchedules` NOC feed, gated `maintenanceManagement:read`) and the Active Maintenance widget are the counterpart, and being the ONLY surface that speaks about planned work makes two otherwise-cosmetic things load-bearing. **It must not borrow the vocabulary of an outage**: `setHeaderCount`'s fallback pill is red — the generic "these are down" count — and a red count over planned work claims something needs doing about the one set of devices where nobody should do anything; maintenance rows carry no alert severity either, so the pill is NEUTRAL via `setHeaderPills` (the same reasoning that paints the Status Summary tile and the Status Map dots purple). **And the asset filter narrows the LIST, never the window**: every other feed's rows ARE devices, so dropping non-matching ones is right, but a maintenance row is a SCHEDULE covering whatever mix its criteria and explicit list matched — narrowing it the ordinary way reports "2 devices" about a window that has taken four down, and the operator sizes the work and the return time against a number Polaris shrank for them. So a schedule survives when ANY of its devices is in scope and is then reported WHOLE (every device counted, every type named), with `matchedCount`/`filtered` carrying the in-scope share ("4 devices (2 in this scope)"); membership comes from open `AssetMaintenanceWindow` rows, so the types named are the ones actually held. **The window end ships in two forms and neither is redundant**: `endsAt` is the SERVER's wall clock, the only form that may be displayed (the recurrence engine runs on that clock — business rule 16 — so re-deriving it in the viewer's zone paints the window on the wrong hour), and `endsAtUtc` is the same instant, the only form a countdown can be computed from. **Every verb is gated where the MODAL is**, not where the widget is: Open schedule… / Disable schedule / + New schedule all reach the Maintenance editor, so they need its `maintenanceManagement:write` and are withheld below it, on `/dash`, and in a library preview — a button opening an editor whose every save 403s is worse than no button. A write made from there must survive being off the Assets page: `fetchAssetsPage` returns early when there is no table to repaint, rather than throwing into a silent unhandled rejection behind a write that succeeded. → [narrative-60-64.md#rule-73](narrative-60-64.md#rule-73)

74. **A field Polaris writes onto a device is budgeted where the operator types it, and the budget is the DEVICE's** — FortiOS holds 255 characters in a DHCP `reserved-address` description, and Polaris spends part of that on the wrapper that makes the entry attributable: `Polaris/<user>: <notes> [<hostname>]`, origin first so a FortiGate admin can see which rows are Polaris's, hostname in brackets at the END so `subnetRefreshService.extractHostnameFromDescription` can recover it after a Polaris-loss rebuild. Everything inside those 255 competes — the username, the hostname and the operator's notes — so the room left for notes is a COMPUTED budget (`reservationNotesBudget`, which derives the wrapper's cost by composing with a one-character note and subtracting it, rather than restating the format a second time), never a constant, and never a `maxlength` on the textarea. Polaris used to cap the composed string at 64 for FortiOS 6.2's 35-char field and truncate anything longer, which was wrong twice over: an operator's comment was silently cut on a production gate with nothing said at either end, and the cut took the trailing `[hostname]` with it — whereupon the extractor's bracket branch stopped matching, its legacy branch matched instead, and the tail of the operator's own notes was rediscovered as the device's HOSTNAME. So the cap is now the device's own (`RESERVED_ADDRESS_DESCRIPTION_MAX`), and over-length input is REFUSED at save time (`assertReservationDescriptionFits`, 400, naming the budget, what was typed and how much to cut) rather than trimmed behind the operator's back — on create in phase 3 beside the MAC and deviceName checks, before the row exists, and on edit before the MAC branch, so neither refusal has touched a device. Three boundaries keep the rule from becoming a nuisance. It applies **only on a push-eligible subnet** — elsewhere `notes` is a `@db.Text` column with no device field behind it. An edit is judged **only when it TOUCHES `hostname` or `notes`**, and against what the update will actually store: a row whose note predates the rule or came from discovery must stay editable, since refusing every save on it strands the row for the operator who only wanted to change its expiry. And the truncating `slice` STAYS, as the backstop for exactly those ungated rows — discovery-authored notes, the retry tick replaying one, the infra auto-reserve pass. The browser mirrors the arithmetic (`public/js/reservation-notes.js`, shared by the IP panel and both mobile sheets) as a live counter under the field, and `tests/unit/reservationNotesBudgetDom.test.ts` asserts the mirror against the server's own function so the two cannot drift; the counter is advisory in every case — the service is what refuses. → [narrative-60-64.md#rule-74](narrative-60-64.md#rule-74)

76. **Access is granted on the network profile the endpoint is actually on, and scoping it counts for nothing while a wider rule stands beside it** — installing the OpenSSH Server capability makes Windows create its own `OpenSSH-Server-In-TCP`, which accepts TCP/22 from ANY source and applies to the **Private profile only**. A domain-joined endpoint is on the Domain profile, so that rule never applies to it: sshd installed, running and unreachable, with nothing in the service or the event log to say why — the silent shape the onboarding generator exists to prevent (business rule 72). The Polaris rule `Polaris SSH (TCP 22)` was already `-Profile Any` and so was never the blocked one; what blocked the fleet was the assumption that Windows' rule agreed with it. And because firewall rules are additive ALLOWS, that rule being enabled also meant `-RemoteAddress <polarisServerIp>` narrowed nothing — port 22 stayed open to every host on a Private network while the header of the generated script said `scopes inbound TCP/22 to <ip>`. So `services/sshOnboardingScript.ts` settles Windows' rule either way: with a server address it **disables** it, leaving the scoped Polaris rule as the only inbound path to sshd; with none it **widens** it from `Private` to `Domain, Private`, which changes the networks the rule applies on and never which sources may connect. Public is deliberately not added — reachable-from-Domain is the defect, an any-source TCP/22 rule on the profile a laptop picks up in an airport is not. Both paths are idempotent (they re-read the rule's own `Profile` / `Enabled`), the lookup is wildcarded by NAME (`OpenSSH-Server-In-*`) because the DisplayName is localized and the suffix is build-dependent, and the detection half still judges no firewall at all: it is not told whether a server address was configured, so either settled state would read as drift half the time. → [narrative-60-64.md#rule-76](narrative-60-64.md#rule-76)

77. **A VIP describes an address; it does not claim it — and the status says every fact it has** — a FortiGate virtual IP is a *third* fact about an address, beside who owns it (`sourceType`) and how the gate hands it out (`dhcpBinding`), and it lives in `Reservation.vipInfo` (business rule 23's split, extended by one column). Four consequences. **A VIP address is claimable.** `isSupersedableByCreate` admits `sourceType: "vip"`, and `releaseSupersededDhcpLeaseAt` releases it with NO device I/O (a VIP row carries no push pointers and is not `dhcp_lease`, so neither unpush branch nor the lease-expiry branch fires) while handing its snapshot back to `persistReservationRow`, which stamps it onto the operator's new row — so the address keeps reporting the VIP it still has. The mapped and realserver addresses behind a VIP are ordinary hosts that want a DHCP reservation, and holding the external one in IPAM is an intent Polaris has no better answer to. `interface_ip` is NOT admitted: an address configured on a live interface genuinely cannot be handed to anything else. **The snapshot surviving the claim is what stops the conflict card**: Phase 3c's `manual` branch now stamps rather than raises when the row already names this same VIP (by `name` + `device`), the exact shape of `reservationBelongsToInfraDevice` on the managed-switch/AP side of the same problem — without it the feature's first effect would be one conflict card per claimed VIP per cycle. **A VIP row records the DHCP fact it used to drop.** `decideVipDhcpBinding` writes `dhcpBinding` onto a `vip` row from a DHCP entry at the same address, keeping `decideInfraDhcpBinding`'s three omissions verbatim — never `sourceType` (only the succession path, which can see the VIP is GONE, may flip it), never `expiresAt`, and `macAddress` fill-only from the entry. **The Status column reports both halves** (`_ipStatusPresentation`, and the PDF and CSV exports read the same function so a record that leaves the screen cannot disagree with the table it left): the VIP is a PREFIX on the allocation — "VIP / Leased", "VIP / Reserved", "VIP / Conflict", `VS` for a load-balance virtual server — and a standalone label is unchanged from what operators already read. The dot is the VIP's purple whenever one is present, except that a conflict and a permanently failed push keep red, because those two are the only states asking somebody to act. The single-winner ladder this replaced is why the rule exists: it reported whichever fact it tested first, so an address with a VIP and a lease read "DHCP Lease", one with a VIP and a conflict read "Conflict", and an operator refused a reservation *because of* a VIP could not find that VIP anywhere in the table. **And the per-subnet discover reads the VIP table.** It is one `/api/v2/cmdb/firewall/vip` call on the same transport as the DHCP reads, parsed by `parseVipRow` (which accepts all three encodings the CMDB row arrives in), settled rather than awaited so a gate that answers for DHCP and refuses the firewall CMDB still completes; a table that could NOT be read skips every VIP decision and says so in the toast and the Event, never "there are no VIPs" (business rule 53). Retirement is scoped to snapshots naming THIS gate — RFC1918 space repeats behind different FortiGates, the same reason business rule 17 scopes ARP evidence per device. → [narrative-60-64.md#rule-77](narrative-60-64.md#rule-77)

78. **An automation may choose to speak for a silenced device, and then it must name who silenced it** — dependency suppression silences every automation about a device behind a dark parent (rules 16 and 37), and exactly ONE thing may opt out of that silence: the `monitorStatus == down` trigger itself, through its own key `alertWhenDependencyDown` (in the trigger JSON beside `missedPolls`, refused anywhere else and inside a multi-condition trigger, read only through `notificationTypes.ruleAlertsWhenDependencyDown`, and not part of `triggerIdentityOf`). An opted-in automation keeps its dependency-suppressed devices in evaluation and **fires on the suppression EDGE** — `resolveAssetStateReadings` hands each such device a synthesized `down` reading flagged `dependencyDown`, because the upstream's confirmed verdict IS the evidence and waiting for the device's own count at half cadence would tell the plant operator late what the pill already says. The alert it raises **says DEPENDENCY DOWN** (the `{dependency.summary}` banner, the ` · DEPENDENCY DOWN` subject tag, `{trigger.summary}` replaced by `dependencyTriggerSummary`, the in-app message the whole sentence) **and names who silenced it**: `dependencyTreeService.resolveDependencyBlame` walks UP from the device — a parent is blamed as `dependency_test` / `maintenance` / `suppressed` / `down` in that order, so a switch that is both suppressed and reading `down` under a dark gate keeps the walk going to the gate — and the alert carries the UPSTREAM device (directly above) and the ROOT CAUSE (dark in its own right; blank in the template when it is the upstream itself, so the row prunes rather than repeats), snapshotted on `Notification.dependencyDown` + `dependencyBlame` so the row explains itself after the tree is recomputed. **The alert's flavour follows the asset's flag**: a live plain Down alert whose device turns Dep. Down, or a dependency-down alert whose upstream is back while the device stays dark, is ENDED (`system:dependency-down` / `system:dependency-released`, audited as `notification.superseded`, NO reset actions — nothing recovered) and raised again in the other flavour, so the operators hear "and it is the switch" or "and now it is the PLC itself". Three things the opt-out does NOT reach: a **maintenance window still silences** (rule 16 wins — announced downtime is not an outage to report); **reminders and escalation still pause** while the asset is suppressed (rule 16's pause in the escalation sweep, unchanged — the operator asked for one notification); and **`clearSuppressedAlerts` never retires a `dependencyDown` row** — the sweep excludes them in its query, because retiring the one alert that is supposed to be live on a suppressed asset would have the engine re-raise it every tick. A blame walk that fails still lets the alert out, worded without a name: "your PLC is dependency down" beats silence even when the switch cannot be named. Every automation WITHOUT the key behaves exactly as before. → [narrative-78.md#rule-78](narrative-78.md#rule-78)

79. **An operator's removal of a MAC is a correction, not a suppression** — `DELETE /assets/:id/macs/:mac` deletes the `AssetMacAddress` row and nothing else. No tombstone, no exclusion list, no source-scoped ownership: if the network reports that address against the same asset again, the next discovery reconcile re-adds it, and that is the designed outcome rather than the removal having failed. Polaris cannot tell a stale association from a live one, and only one of them should stick — a MAC inherited from a bad merge or a decommissioned DHCP lease is never seen again and stays gone, while a MAC that keeps returning is being transmitted right now (a shared dock, a ZTNA-relayed identity), which is a fact about the network the operator needs to see rather than a record to paper over. A suppression would turn the second case into a permanently wrong asset record that looks correct. Two obligations follow. The confirm must SAY the address may come back — a control that silently fails to stick reads as a bug. And the primary-MAC recompute that follows a removal goes through `utils/macAddresses.ts → selectPrimaryMac()` rather than a local freshest-wins sort, so hardware-truth sources still outrank sightings and a `[mac, macEnd]` range row — a port block, not a device identity — is never promoted into `Asset.macAddress`. The grant is `assets:write`: correcting inventory is the assets administrator's act, and the browser control must name that same key (it read `subnets:fullwrite` until 2026-09-21, which hid the button from the only built-in role whose job this is). → [narrative-60-64.md#rule-79](narrative-60-64.md#rule-79)

80. **Downtime Polaris itself causes is not an incident — and a silence it grants expires on its own** — an agent upgrade, reinstall or uninstall stops the agent service on the host, which drops the WebSocket, which writes `agent.disconnected` at WARNING level, which is exactly what the seeded baseline automation watches. The operator asked for the work and got paged for it. Nothing in the notification path knew an operation was in flight: `installStatus` said `"upgrading"` and no trigger gate has ever read it. So `services/agentInstallService.ts` now takes a **MaintenanceHold** on the asset for the duration (`services/maintenanceScheduleService.ts` → `openMaintenanceHold` / `releaseMaintenanceHold`), which suppresses it through the gate every trigger already honours — `assetCanTrigger` is `monitored && !maintenance && !dependencySuppressed`, and event automations honour it as of the monitored-gate widening (business rule 37). A hold is a HOLDER, not a window: the reconcile closes any open window it cannot re-derive from a live holder, so a hand-made window row would be torn down within 30s of being taken — the hold row is the "should be in maintenance" input the diff runs against, which is also what makes entry go through the one path that parks the status, writes `maintenance.entered` and sweeps live alerts (business rule 16). **Which operations, and why not the others:** upgrade, reinstall and uninstall stop a RUNNING agent; a first install and a `/retry` of a failed one have no agent to disconnect, and holding there would silence a host that is still telling the truth about itself. **Release is on REATTACH, not on the installer returning** (`services/agentChannelService.ts` → `attach`): the WS teardown trails the service stop by up to a heartbeat interval (30s ping, one missed pong), so a hold dropped when the script exited lets the disconnect through by seconds. An uninstall is the exception — nothing is coming back, so completing it ends the hold. A FAILURE releases immediately: an agent that is down because its upgrade failed is a real problem and the alert about it is the point. **And every hold carries `expiresAt`** (20 min upgrade / uninstall, 30 min reinstall), enforced by the reconcile rather than by whoever forgot to release: a held asset is not being watched, so a release that never runs — process died mid-upgrade, host never came back — would otherwise stop monitoring a production machine forever and leave nothing saying why. The expiry closes the window with `endReason: "expired"` and logs a warning; the suppressed alert then fires late instead of never. The operator and the decommission sweep both outrank a hold (`operatorReleaseAsset` / `releaseAssetsForDecommission` delete the holder, or the next tick would re-open the window and make their action look ignored). → [narrative-80.md#rule-80](narrative-80.md#rule-80)

82. **A measurement of the host must not be dominated by the measurer, and a scheduling offset is not a way to protect one** — the agent's host CPU figure spans the WHOLE telemetry cadence and is never a sampled window: `internal/collectors/cputimes.go` reads the kernel's cumulative per-core counters each pass and reports the delta since the previous one, so the loop's cadence IS the averaging window and `TelemetryOnce` never blocks. It was `cpu.Percent(1*time.Second, true)` until agent 0.20.0 — one second measured in every sixty, which is not noise but BIAS, and the bias is the measurer's own: anything the agent did inside that second entered the chart magnified ~60×, and on a **single-vCPU VM** the agent's own work is the entire core, so a `processConnections` sweep (phase 57) or a `tasklist /svc` (phase 42) still running when the window opened reported ~100% CPU for an otherwise idle host, the same way every minute. **The 0.19.0 loop phase-offsets could not have fixed this and must never be relied on to**: a phase staggers when a pass STARTS and says nothing about how long it runs, and on the single-core hosts where this matters every pass runs long — `processConnections` only had to overrun by 11 s to reach the window. The window was DELETED, not moved; the general form is that a measurement needing the process to be quiet is a measurement that is wrong, because the process cannot promise to be quiet. Three things a change here gets wrong by default. **Not `cpu.Percent(0, …)`** despite it having exactly this semantic — gopsutil keeps its last-call state in a package global shared by every caller in the process, so a second collector calling `Percent` would consume this one's baseline and silently shrink the window back, invisibly to any test on `cputimes.go`; the baseline is ours in `cpuSampler`, and gopsutil's arithmetic (idle and iowait are not busy; Linux guest time is not double-counted, the kernel already folding it into user) is reproduced locally. **The aggregate is the summed-delta ratio, not the mean of the per-core vector** — they agree only when every core advanced equally, and the summed form survives a parked core and a vector truncated at `maxReportedCores`. **Four conditions fall back to one short (250 ms) blocking read** (no baseline, a changed logical-core count, counters that did not advance, counters that went backwards after a snapshot restore or resume), none expected in normal operation since the package `init()` primes the baseline at process start. Per-pinned-program telemetry deliberately KEEPS its prime→sleep(300 ms)→read window: a per-process percentage is that process's CPU time over wall time, so a colliding collector depresses it rather than inflating it, and a per-PID baseline cannot be carried across passes when PIDs come and go. Operator-visible: the chart is flatter and CPU thresholds fire on a sustained average rather than a lucky sample; an installed agent keeps the old behaviour until upgraded. → [narrative-82.md#rule-82](narrative-82.md#rule-82)
